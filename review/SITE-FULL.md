## Secure Your iPhone: A Step-by-Step Guide

### What this is

Nobody has switched on this iPhone's security settings for you. On a company-managed phone, IT does that with a management system (called MDM). On yours, you'll do it yourself with this guide.

You don't need to be "good with tech." Every step tells you exactly what to tap. Words in **bold** are the words you'll see on screen. A path like **Settings > General > Software Update** means: open the Settings app, tap **General**, then tap **Software Update**.

This guide is written for **iOS 26**. If your phone still runs **iOS 18**, most steps are the same. Where a menu has a different name on iOS 18, the guide says so.

**How long it takes:** about 45 to 60 minutes for the Must-do part, plus 20 to 30 minutes for the Nice-to-have part. You don't have to do it all at once. Tick things off as you go.

**What to have ready:**

- Your iPhone, charged above 50% or plugged in.
- Wi-Fi.
- Your **Apple Account password**. (This used to be called your Apple ID. It's the email and password you use for the App Store and iCloud.)
- Your **phone carrier login**: the username and password for your mobile provider's website or app (for example, the company you pay your phone bill to). You need it for step 11, to lock your phone number.
- Your phone's **passcode**: the code you type to unlock it.
- About 10 minutes of quiet time for the Apple Account steps.
- **Somewhere familiar, like home.** On iOS 26.4 and later, a feature called Stolen Device Protection (step 8) is often already on. It makes some changes wait an hour when you're somewhere your phone doesn't know well. Doing the Must-do part at home (or your usual workplace) avoids most of that.

**Please do the steps in order.** The order matters. For example, you turn on backups before the setting that can wipe the phone, and you finish your Apple Account changes before the setting that adds a one-hour wait to them.

**One thing to know first:** some settings ask for Face ID or your passcode, and some may make you wait an hour. This is a safety feature called Security Delay, explained in step 8. That's normal.

Some steps end with a **Going further** box. It stays closed until you tap it, and holds extra protection for people who are comfortable with tech. Everyone else can skip these boxes: the main steps already protect you well.

### Before you start: update your iPhone

**Why this matters:** Updates fix the security holes that criminals use. An out-of-date phone is the easiest kind to break into.

**Steps**

1. Plug your iPhone into a charger and connect to Wi-Fi.
2. Go to **Settings > General > Software Update**.
3. If you see **Update Now** or **Upgrade to iOS 26**, tap it. Then follow the prompts and enter your passcode.
4. Wait. The phone restarts on its own. This can take 15 to 45 minutes.
5. Go back to **Settings > General > Software Update** and check again, because sometimes a second update shows up.

**How to check it worked:** **Settings > General > Software Update** says **iOS is up to date**. You can also check **Settings > General > About > iOS Version**.

**If you don't see this:** If your phone can't get iOS 26, it may be too old. Install the newest update it offers and tell IT the model, which is shown in **Settings > General > About > Model Name**.

**Going further:**
- **Don't stay on the older version.** When a new big version comes out, **Software Update** may also offer an update for the version you already have. Take the newest version. Apple only promises to fix every known security hole in the latest version. Trade-off: big updates sometimes move menus around for a while.
- **Know when your phone stops getting fixes.** iOS 26 needs an iPhone 11 or newer. The website **endoflife.date** shows how long each iPhone has been getting updates. A phone that's no longer supported still works, but new holes in it are never fixed. Plan to replace it, and move banking and work email to a supported device as soon as you can.
- **Update your other Apple devices too.** Your iPad, Mac and Apple Watch share your Apple Account with this phone. If one is out of date, update it. If you no longer use it, remove it from your account (step 6c). Trade-off: a very old iPad may have no update left, so removing it may be the only option.

## Part 1: Must-do (everyone)

### 1. Turn on all Automatic Updates

**Why this matters:** You won't always remember to update. With this on, your phone fixes itself overnight while it charges.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > General > Software Update > Automatic Updates**.
2. Under **iOS Updates**, turn on **Automatically Download** and **Automatically Install**.
3. Turn on **System Files** (the switch may be shown as **Automatically Install** under a **System Files** heading). On iOS 18 this is called **Security Responses & System Files**.
4. Go back to the main Settings screen. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Background Security Improvements** and make sure **Automatically Install** is on. (This is new in iOS 26.1. It sends urgent security fixes between normal updates.)

**How to check it worked:** All the switches above are green.

**If you don't see this:** If there's no **Background Security Improvements** option, your phone is on iOS 26.0 or older. Update it (see "Before you start") and look again.

**Heads-up:** Your phone installs updates overnight and restarts, so leave it charging and on Wi-Fi overnight. If you add a SIM PIN later (step 11), you'll need to type it after these restarts.

**Going further:**
- **Install urgent fixes the same day.** Automatic updates can reach your phone days or even weeks after an update comes out, because Apple releases them in waves. When Apple's security notes at **support.apple.com/100100** say a problem "may have been exploited", go to **Settings > General > Software Update** and install it now.
- **Restart your phone once a week.** Some spyware only lives in the phone's short-term memory, and a restart clears it. Since iOS 18.1, a phone that stays locked for about three days also restarts itself, which puts it back in its most locked state. Trade-off: if you set a SIM PIN (step 11), you'll type it after every restart.
- **Keep test versions off this phone.** Go to **Settings > General > Software Update > Beta Updates** and make sure it says **Off**. Test ("beta") versions can be unstable and may have holes that aren't fixed yet. Trade-off: you get new features a few months later than people who test.

### 2. Turn on backups

**Why this matters:** If your phone is lost, stolen, broken, or wiped, a backup gets your photos, messages and apps back onto a new phone. Do this **before** step 3, which can wipe the phone after too many wrong passcodes.

**Steps (iCloud, the easiest way)**

1. Go to **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**.
2. Tap **iCloud Backup**.
3. Turn on **Back Up This iPhone**. (On older versions it's simply called **iCloud Backup**.)
4. Tap **Back Up Now** to make your first backup. Stay on Wi-Fi until it finishes.

**How to check it worked:** Under **Back Up Now**, you see **Last successful backup** with today's date.

**If you don't have enough iCloud storage:** Your phone will say so. You can buy more storage (iCloud+), or back up to your own computer instead:
- When you plug your iPhone into your computer, it asks **Trust This Computer?** Tap **Trust** and enter your passcode. That's fine here, because it's **your own** computer. (Step 16 explains why you shouldn't do this on other computers.)
- **Mac:** plug in the iPhone, open **Finder**, click your iPhone in the sidebar, choose **Back up all of the data on your iPhone to this Mac**, tick **Encrypt local backup**, set a password, and click **Back Up Now**.
- **Windows:** use the **Apple Devices** app (or iTunes on older PCs) and tick **Encrypt local backup** the same way. If you don't already have the Apple Devices app, **ask IT before installing it**.
- Write down that backup password. Without it, you can't restore the backup.

**Heads-up:** A computer backup only happens when you plug in and run it, so iCloud is better for most people.

**Going further:**
- **Keep a second copy on your own computer.** Even with iCloud Backup on, make an **encrypted** computer backup every month or two, as described above. An encrypted backup also keeps your saved passwords, Wi-Fi settings and Health data. Trade-off: if you forget that backup password, the backup is useless.
- **Syncing isn't a backup.** With **iCloud Photos** on, deleting a photo on one device deletes it everywhere. You get 30 days to rescue it from **Recently Deleted**, then it's gone. Now and then, copy your most precious photos to a computer or an external drive.
- **Check what your backup includes.** Go to **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**, tap **Manage Storage** (or **Manage Account Storage**), then **Backups**, then this iPhone. Make sure the apps whose data matters to you are switched on. Trade-off: more apps means more iCloud storage used.
- **Know who can read it.** iCloud backups are encrypted, but Apple holds the keys unless you turn on **Advanced Data Protection** (step 30). Only do that if IT asks, because of its downsides.

### 3. Use a strong passcode and turn on Erase Data

**Why this matters:** Your passcode is the front door to your phone. Codes like 1234, 0000, 111111 or your birthday are the first ones a thief tries.

**Do step 2 (backups) first.** Erase Data can wipe your phone, so make sure a backup exists.

**Steps: change to a strong passcode**

1. Go to **Settings > Face ID & Passcode** (on phones with a Home button: **Touch ID & Passcode**).
2. Enter your current passcode.
3. Tap **Change Passcode** and enter your current passcode again.
4. Tap **Passcode Options**. Choose **6-Digit Numeric Code** at least, or **Custom Alphanumeric Code** (letters and numbers) for even better protection. Don't pick **4-Digit Numeric Code**.
5. Type your new passcode twice.

**Heads-up:** If your phone says you have to wait an hour, Stolen Device Protection is already on. That's normal. Carry on with step 4 and come back to this step when the hour is up.

A good passcode has no patterns (not 123456 or 135790), no repeated digits, and nothing someone could guess, like a birthday, an address or a phone number.

**Remember your new passcode:**
- Write it down somewhere safe (not on the phone, and not in your phone case) for the first few days.
- Lock and unlock your phone a few times with the passcode, not Face ID, to practise.
- If you forget it in the first 72 hours: after a few wrong tries the screen says **iPhone Unavailable**. Tap **Forgot Passcode?** at the bottom, then **Enter Previous Passcode**, and type your **old** passcode. Then set a new one.

**Not sure you'll remember the new code yet?** It's fine to leave Erase Data for a few days. Use the new passcode until you know it by heart, then come back and do this part.

**Steps: Erase Data after 10 wrong tries**

1. Still in **Settings > Face ID & Passcode**, scroll to the very bottom.
2. Turn on **Erase Data**, then tap **Enable**.

**How to check it worked:** Lock your phone. The unlock screen asks for 6 digits (or shows a keyboard). In **Face ID & Passcode**, **Erase Data** is green.

**Heads-up:** After 10 wrong passcodes in a row, the phone wipes itself. The phone makes you wait longer and longer between wrong tries, so this almost never happens by accident. But if small children play with your phone, keep it away from them.

**Going further:**
- **Use letters, not just numbers.** A 6-digit code has a million possible combinations. Phone-cracking tools try number codes first. A **Custom Alphanumeric Code** of 10 or more characters, like three or four unrelated words, takes them vastly longer. Trade-off: it's slower to type, but Face ID handles most unlocks. You'll mainly type it after a restart, or when you haven't unlocked for a while.
- **Hide the code when you type it.** Many phone thefts with a known passcode start with someone watching you type it in a bar or on a train. In public, use Face ID, and cover the screen with your hand when you must type the code.
- **Change it if someone may have seen it.** Don't wait to find out. Then go to **Settings > Face ID & Passcode** and tap **Expire Previous Passcode Now**. Otherwise the old code still works for 72 hours, which is a safety net if you forget the new one, but also a way in for whoever saw it. With Stolen Device Protection set to **Always** (step 8), changing the passcode makes you wait an hour. That's normal.
- **Keep it unique.** Don't reuse it as your SIM PIN, bank card PIN or door code. If one leaks, the others stay safe.

### 4. Set Auto-Lock to 2 minutes or less

**Why this matters:** If you put your phone down and walk away, it locks itself before someone else can pick it up and use it.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Display & Brightness > Auto-Lock**.
2. Choose **2 minutes** or less. If it already says **30 seconds** or **1 minute**, leave it. Don't make it longer.

**How to check it worked:** **Auto-Lock** shows the time you picked. Leave your phone alone, and the screen should go dark after that time.

**Heads-up:** If you see that Auto-Lock is greyed out at 30 seconds, Low Power Mode is on. That's fine. It's even safer.

**Going further:**
- **Lock it yourself every time.** Press the side button whenever you put the phone down, even for a moment. Auto-Lock is the safety net, not the habit.
- **Lock to one app when you hand it over.** **Guided Access** keeps the phone inside a single app, for example when a child plays a game or you show someone a photo. Turn it on in **Settings > Accessibility > Guided Access**, set a passcode in **Passcode Settings**, then triple-click the side button (or Home button) inside the app. Trade-off: triple-click now also starts Guided Access. That's fine, because you set it up yourself (step 26 is only about features you didn't set up).

### 5. Check Face ID (or Touch ID)

**Why this matters:** Face ID should only open your phone when you're looking at it, awake. Someone else's face also shouldn't be saved on it.

**Steps: Require Attention**

1. Go to **Settings > Face ID & Passcode** and enter your passcode.
2. Turn on **Require Attention for Face ID**.

**Steps: check for an unknown face**

1. Still in **Face ID & Passcode**, look under the Face ID section.
2. If you see **Set Up an Alternate Appearance**, good. Only one face is saved. Don't set one up unless you really need it.
3. If you **don't** see that button, a second face (an "alternate appearance") is already saved. If you didn't add it yourself, tap **Reset Face ID**, then tap **Set Up Face ID** and scan only your own face. (If you're asked to wait an hour, that's Stolen Device Protection. See step 8.)

**If you don't see this (phones with a Home button):** Go to **Settings > Touch ID & Passcode**. Look at the list of fingerprints. Swipe left on any you don't recognise and tap **Delete**. You can rename them by tapping a fingerprint, so you know which finger is which.

**How to check it worked:** **Require Attention for Face ID** is green. Close your eyes and try to unlock with Face ID. It shouldn't work.

**Heads-up:** If you wear sunglasses that block your eyes, Face ID may not work with Require Attention on. Just use your passcode then.

**Going further:**
- **Switch off Face ID in two seconds.** Press and hold the side button and either volume button until **slide to power off** appears, then tap **Cancel**. Now the phone needs your passcode before Face ID works again. Use this when someone might push the phone at your face. Let go as soon as the slider appears: holding longer starts an **Emergency SOS** countdown that can call emergency services.
- **Lock your most private apps (iOS 18 and later).** Touch and hold an app's icon, tap **Require Face ID**, and confirm. Good choices are email, banking and **Photos**. (The **Passwords** app already asks for your face every time.) Then someone you've handed your unlocked phone to can't open them. Trade-off: if Face ID fails, the app accepts your phone passcode instead, so this doesn't stop someone who knows your passcode. If you choose **Hide and Require Face ID**, that app's notifications stop showing.
- **Check your private photo albums are locked.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Photos** (on iOS 17: **Settings > Photos**) and make sure **Use Face ID** is on. The **Hidden** and **Recently Deleted** albums then need your face or passcode.
- **Turn off Face ID with a Mask if you don't need it.** In **Settings > Face ID & Passcode**, turn off **Face ID with a Mask**. Apple says Face ID is most accurate with full-face recognition. Trade-off: with a mask on, you'll type your passcode.

### 6. Lock down your Apple Account

**Why this matters:** Your Apple Account holds your photos, messages, passwords and backups. If someone gets into it, they can see all of it, and they can even lock you out of your own phone.

**Heads-up before you start:** If **Stolen Device Protection** (step 8) is already on, some changes below (removing a phone number or device, changing your password, adding a Recovery Contact) may make you wait an hour, especially away from home. If that happens, carry on with the other steps and come back later.

#### 6a. Make sure two-factor authentication is on

Two-factor means that when you sign in on a new device, Apple also sends a code to your phone. A password alone isn't enough.

1. Go to **Settings** and tap **your name** at the top.
2. Tap **Sign-In & Security**. (On older versions this is **Password & Security**.)
3. Look at **Two-Factor Authentication**. It should say **On**.
4. If it says **Off**, tap it, then tap **Turn On Two-Factor Authentication** and follow the prompts.

#### 6b. Check your trusted phone numbers

These are the numbers Apple sends sign-in codes to.

1. In **Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security**, look at the list of phone numbers (tap **Two-Factor Authentication** if you don't see them).
2. Make sure every number is yours, or belongs to someone you really trust.
3. To remove one, tap **Edit**, then tap the red minus next to the number.
4. It's a good idea to add a second number you control, like a home phone or a partner's phone, so you can still get codes if you lose your phone.

#### 6c. Check which devices are signed in

1. Go to **Settings > [your name]** and scroll to the bottom. You'll see a list of devices signed in with your Apple Account.
2. Tap any device you don't recognise, or one you sold or gave away.
3. Tap **Remove from Account**, then confirm.
4. If you removed something you didn't recognise, change your Apple Account password afterwards: **Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Change Password**.

#### 6d. Add a Recovery Contact

A Recovery Contact is a person you trust who can help you get back into your account if you forget your password. They can't see your data. They can only give you a code.

1. Go to **Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Recovery Contacts**.
2. Tap **Add Recovery Contact**.
3. Pick someone from the list or tap **Choose Someone Else**. They need an Apple device (iPhone, iPad or Mac) that's up to date, with iMessage turned on. You need iMessage on too, and you both need to be over 13.
4. Follow the prompts. They'll get a message to accept. (Family members in your Family Sharing group are added straight away.)

**How to check it worked:** Two-factor says **On**. Every phone number and device in the list is yours. Your Recovery Contact shows in the list once they accept.

**Heads-up:** Removing a device signs it out of iCloud. If it was an old phone you still use for something, it will ask for the password next time.

**Going further:**
- **Add more than one Recovery Contact.** You can have up to five. Choose people who don't live with you, so a fire or a burglary can't take out everyone at once. Trade-off: with Stolen Device Protection on, each change may make you wait an hour.
- **Clean up Sign in with Apple.** Go to **Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Sign in with Apple** (on some versions it's directly under your name). Tap any app you no longer use and choose **Stop Using**. Trade-off: if you go back to that app, you may need to make a new account.
- **Keep your real email address private (iCloud+).** With a paid iCloud+ plan, **Hide My Email** (in **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**, or search Settings for it) makes a random address that forwards to you. Use it for shops and newsletters, so your real address, and your Apple Account email, appear in fewer leaks and scam lists. Trade-off: you have more addresses to keep track of.
- **Know the strongest options.** **Advanced Data Protection** (step 30) and **security keys** (step 31) lock your account down further. They have real downsides. Only turn on Advanced Data Protection if IT asks you to. Security keys on a personal account are your choice, after you read the downsides in step 31.

### 7. Turn on Find My

**Why this matters:** If you lose your phone, Find My shows it on a map, makes it play a sound, and lets you lock it or wipe it from another device.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > [your name] > Find My**.
2. Tap **Find My iPhone**.
3. Turn on **Find My iPhone**.
4. Turn on **Find My network**. This lets your phone be found even when it's offline or switched off for a while.
5. Turn on **Send Last Location**. This sends the phone's location to Apple just before the battery dies.

**How to check it worked:** All three switches are green. On another device or a computer, go to **icloud.com/find**, sign in, and you should see your iPhone on the map.

**Heads-up:** With Find My on, nobody can wipe and resell your phone without your Apple Account password. If you ever sell or give away the phone, sign out of your Apple Account first.

**Going further:**
- **Leave "Findable After Power Off" on.** On most models from iPhone 11 onwards, the power-off screen shows **iPhone Findable After Power Off**. That means the phone can still be found for a while after a thief switches it off. It relies on **Find My network**, so keep that switched on.
- **Know your Apple Account password by heart, or on paper at home.** To use **icloud.com/find** from another device, you need that password. A password that's only saved on the lost phone won't help you. The practice in step 17 checks this.
- **Share your location with one trusted person.** In the **Find My** app, tap **People**, then **+** and **Share My Location**, and pick a partner or family member. (If it says location sharing is off, tap **Me** and turn on **Share My Location** first.) They can then help you find your phone if you're stuck without it. Trade-off: they can see where you are all the time, so review who sees it now and then with **Safety Check** (step 18).

### 8. Turn on Stolen Device Protection (set to "Always")

**Why this matters:** Sometimes a thief watches you type your passcode, then steals your phone. Stolen Device Protection stops them from changing your Apple Account password with just the passcode. They also need your face, and for the most important changes, they have to wait an hour. While it's on, nobody can turn off Find My at all.

This step comes after steps 6 and 7 on purpose. Once it's set to **Always**, Apple Account changes take an hour, so it's easier to make them first.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Face ID & Passcode** and enter your passcode.
2. Scroll down and tap **Stolen Device Protection**.
3. Turn on **Stolen Device Protection** if it isn't on already.
4. Under **Require Security Delay**, choose **Always**.

**How to check it worked:** The **Stolen Device Protection** screen shows it as on, with **Always** ticked.

**If you don't see this, or it won't turn on:** It needs all of these:
- Two-factor authentication on (step 6a).
- A passcode (step 3) and Face ID or Touch ID (step 5).
- Find My on (step 7).
- **Significant Locations** on: go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services > System Services > Significant Locations** and turn it on. (The main **Location Services** switch must be on too.)

**Heads-up:**
- With **Always** chosen, some changes make you **wait one hour** and then use Face ID again, even at home. These include changing your Apple Account password or your passcode, changing trusted phone numbers, devices or Recovery Contacts, and adding or removing Face ID. That's the point. It's annoying for you, and much more annoying for a thief.
- Before you take your phone for repair, sell it, or move to a new phone, turn this off first. Turning it off can also need the one-hour wait, so plan ahead. When your phone comes back from repair, turn it on again and choose **Always**.

**Going further:**
- **Close the gap it leaves open.** Stolen Device Protection guards your Apple Account, settings and saved passwords. But a thief who knows your passcode can still open apps that only use the phone's own lock. For banking, email and authenticator apps, turn on the app's own PIN or password lock in its settings, and make it different from your phone passcode. Trade-off: one more code to remember.
- **Turn on your bank's alerts.** In your banking app, turn on alerts for every payment and every new device that signs in. Then you hear about a thief quickly, even if the phone is gone. Trade-off: more notifications.

### 9. Limit what works on the Lock Screen

**Why this matters:** Some things work even while your phone is locked. A stranger holding your locked phone could switch on Airplane Mode (so you can't track it), reply to your messages, or unlock your smart front door.

#### 9a. Allow Access When Locked

1. Go to **Settings > Face ID & Passcode** and enter your passcode.
2. Scroll to **Allow Access When Locked**.
3. Turn **off** these:
   - **Control Center**: stops a thief from switching on Airplane Mode or turning off Wi-Fi to hide the phone.
   - **Reply with Message**: stops people replying to your messages from the Lock Screen.
   - **Home Control**: stops people controlling your smart home, like locks, lights or garage doors.
   - **Accessories** (iOS 18 only): keep it **off**. On iOS 26 this switch isn't here any more. See 9b instead.
4. The rest is **your choice**. These include **Wallet**, **Siri**, **Notification Center**, **Today View and Search**, **Lock Screen Widgets**, **Live Activities** and **Return Missed Calls**. Turning more off is safer. Leaving some on is more convenient. For example, keeping **Wallet** on lets you show a boarding pass quickly. Apple Pay still needs your face or passcode to pay. (The one exception is a transit card or student ID set to **Express Mode**, which works without Face ID.) Turning off **Siri** means nobody can ask your locked phone to do things for them.

#### 9b. Wired accessories (iOS 26)

This controls whether things you plug in with a cable (a computer, a car, a USB gadget) can talk to your phone.

1. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Scroll down to the **Security** section and tap **Wired Accessories**.
3. Choose **Ask for New Accessories** (or **Always Ask** if you want to approve every time).
4. If you only see **Automatically Allow When Unlocked** and **Always Allow** (phones with a Lightning port do), keep **Automatically Allow When Unlocked**.
5. Never choose **Always Allow**.

**How to check it worked:** Lock your phone. Swipe down from the top right corner (on phones with a Home button, swipe up from the bottom edge). Control Center should not open. In **Wired Accessories**, your choice has a tick.

**Heads-up:**
- You'll need to unlock your phone to use Control Center (the flashlight, for example). Just look at the phone first, then swipe.
- Charging from a normal wall plug still works. A wired car system, dock or USB gadget may ask you to unlock the phone or approve it first.

**Going further:**
- **Go strict.** In **Allow Access When Locked**, turn off everything except **Return Missed Calls**, if you use it. That includes **Siri**, **Wallet**, **Notification Center**, **Today View and Search**, **Lock Screen Widgets** and **Live Activities**. A locked phone then shows almost nothing. Emergency calls and Medical ID still work. Trade-off: you'll unlock for a boarding pass, a timer or a delivery update.
- **Pick Always Ask for wired accessories (iOS 26).** In **Settings > Privacy & Security > Wired Accessories**, **Always Ask** means even a car or dock you used before needs your approval each time. Trade-off: one extra tap whenever you plug into the car.
- **Check what your Lock Screen shows.** Remove widgets that show calendar details, reminders or messages. Touch and hold the Lock Screen, tap **Customize**, then **Lock Screen**, and remove what you don't want strangers to see.
- **Keep Medical ID, but keep it short.** In the **Health** app, tap your picture, then **Medical ID > Edit**, and leave **Show When Locked** on, so rescuers can see your allergies and emergency contacts. Trade-off: anyone holding your phone can read it, so leave out things a stranger doesn't need, like your home address.

### 10. Hide message previews on the Lock Screen

**Why this matters:** Messages often include login codes and private things. Anyone near your locked phone can read them if previews show.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Notifications**.
2. Tap **Show Previews**.
3. Choose **When Unlocked**.

**How to check it worked:** Ask someone to text you while your phone is locked. You'll see who it's from, but not what it says. When you look at the phone, Face ID unlocks it and the text appears.

**Heads-up:** On phones with Face ID this is barely noticeable, because the preview appears as soon as you look at the phone.

**Going further:**
- **Go further for sensitive apps.** For email, banking and authenticator apps, go to **Settings > Notifications**, tap the app, and set **Show Previews** to **Never**. Then their messages stay hidden even while the phone is unlocked on a desk. Trade-off: you open the app to see what arrived.
- **Clear old login codes automatically.** Go to **Settings > General > AutoFill & Passwords** and, under **Verification Codes**, turn on **Delete After Use**. Codes that your phone fills in for you are then removed from Messages and Mail. Trade-off: it only works for codes the phone fills in itself, not ones you type by hand.
- **Stop messages being read aloud.** If you use AirPods or CarPlay, **Announce Notifications** can read your texts out loud to anyone nearby. Check it in **Settings > Notifications > Announce Notifications**. Trade-off: you'll have to look at the phone to read messages.

### 11. Lock your phone number with your carrier (and add a SIM PIN if you have a SIM card)

**Why this matters:** Criminals can steal your phone number. They either take the SIM card out of a stolen phone, or they call your carrier pretending to be you and ask for a new SIM. This is called a "SIM swap." Once they have your number, they get your text-message login codes. A carrier lock (11b) stops the phone-call trick, and everyone should turn it on. A SIM PIN (11a) stops the stolen-card trick, so it only matters if your phone has a SIM card you can take out.

#### 11a. Turn on SIM PIN (only if your phone has a SIM card)

**Does your phone have a SIM card?** Look at the side of your iPhone for a small tray with a tiny pinhole. iPhones sold in the US since the iPhone 14 don't have one. They use an eSIM, which is built in and can't be pulled out. **No tray? Skip to 11b.**

**Before you start, read this:**
- Your SIM card already has a **default PIN** from your carrier (often 0000 or 1111, but it varies). **Look it up first** on your carrier's website or app, or call them. **Don't guess.**
- If you type the wrong SIM PIN **3 times**, the SIM locks. You'll then need a longer code called a **PUK** from your carrier to unlock it. Get your PUK from your carrier's website or app, or by calling them, and keep it somewhere safe. Too many wrong PUK tries can kill the SIM for good, and you'd need a new one from the carrier.
- **Think about who needs to reach you.** After every restart, including the overnight ones after updates, your phone can't make or get normal calls and texts until you type the SIM PIN. If family rely on reaching you by phone and you might not notice, you can skip 11a. Do 11b either way.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Cellular** (in some countries it's called **Mobile Service** or **Mobile Data**).
2. Tap **SIM PIN**. If you have more than one line, tap the line first, then **SIM PIN**.
3. Turn on **SIM PIN**.
4. Enter your carrier's default PIN and tap **Done**.
5. Tap **Change PIN**. Enter the default PIN, then a new 4 to 8 digit PIN that only you know. Don't use your phone passcode.
6. Write the new SIM PIN and your PUK somewhere safe that you can reach without this phone, such as a password manager on your computer or a note at home.

**How to check it worked:** Restart your phone:
- **Face ID phones:** press and hold the side button and either volume button until you see **slide to power off**. Drag the slider. Wait 30 seconds, then press and hold the side button until the Apple logo appears.
- **Phones with a Home button:** press and hold the side (or top) button until you see the slider, drag it, then press and hold the same button to turn it back on.

After it starts, the phone asks for your SIM PIN before it connects to the mobile network.

**If you don't see this:** Some carriers hide **SIM PIN** for eSIMs (a SIM with no physical card). If you can't find it, the carrier lock in step 11b matters even more.

**Heads-up:** You'll type the SIM PIN every time your phone restarts, **including the overnight restarts after automatic updates** (step 1). Until you do, calls and mobile data won't work (Wi-Fi still will). If you wake up to **SIM Locked** or **No Service**, unlock your phone and type your SIM PIN.

#### 11b. Lock your account with your carrier

Every carrier does this differently, and they change the names often. Here's what to look for:

1. Sign in to your carrier's website or app.
2. Look for a section called something like **Security**, **Profile**, **Account settings** or **Privacy**.
3. Turn on anything called **SIM protection**, **number lock**, **port-out protection**, **number transfer lock** or **account lock**. This stops anyone moving your number to another SIM or another carrier without extra checks.
4. Set an **account PIN** or **passcode**. This is different from your SIM PIN. The carrier asks for it when anyone calls or visits a shop about your account. Don't use your birthday or your phone passcode.

For example, the big US carriers offer features with names like "Number Lock," "SIM Protection" or "Wireless Account Lock," plus an account PIN or passcode. If you can't find these, call your carrier and say: *"I'd like to add SIM swap protection, a number transfer lock, and an account PIN."*

**How to check it worked:** Your carrier's site or app shows the protection as on, and you have an account PIN saved somewhere safe.

**Heads-up:** When you genuinely want a new phone or a new carrier, you'll need to switch the lock off first. That can take a few minutes, or a day with some carriers.

**Going further:**
- **Make sure a stolen number can't unlock your accounts.** A SIM swap only hurts accounts that send sign-in codes by text message. Move your email, bank and other key accounts to a **passkey** or an **authenticator app** wherever they offer one. The **Staying safe online guide, steps 15 and 16** show how. The trade-off: you need a backup plan, like saved backup codes, in case you lose your phone.
- **Send your carrier's alerts to your email too.** Many carriers send a warning when your SIM or account is changed. If those warnings only go to your phone by text, a criminal who has your number gets them instead of you. In your carrier's app or website, check that your current email address is saved and that account-change alerts are on.
- **Consider an eSIM (if your phone still has a SIM card).** An eSIM is built into the phone, so a thief can't pull it out and put it in another phone. Ask your carrier if they offer one. The trade-off: moving to a new phone takes a few extra steps, and some carriers then hide **SIM PIN**, so the carrier lock in 11b matters even more.
- **Treat sudden "No Service" as an alarm.** If your phone suddenly shows **No Service** or **SOS** in a place where it normally works, and it hasn't just restarted (when it may be waiting for a SIM PIN), your number may have been moved. Call your carrier straight away from another phone, using the number on their website or your bill, not one from a text.

### 12. Set AirDrop to Contacts Only

**Why this matters:** If AirDrop is set to everyone, strangers nearby (on a train, say) can send you pictures or files you didn't ask for.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > General > AirDrop**.
2. Choose **Contacts Only**. If you never use AirDrop, choose **Receiving Off**.

**How to check it worked:** **Contacts Only** (or **Receiving Off**) has a tick.

**Heads-up:** If you need to get a file from someone who isn't in your contacts, you can pick **Everyone for 10 Minutes**. It switches back to **Contacts Only** on its own after 10 minutes. With **Contacts Only**, the other person has to be saved in your Contacts with the email or phone number they use for their Apple Account.

**Going further:**
- **Keep receiving off, and switch it on only when you need it.** Choose **Receiving Off** in **Settings > General > AirDrop**. When someone wants to send you something, unlock your phone, open Control Center, press and hold the box with the Wi-Fi button, tap **AirDrop**, and pick **Contacts Only** or **Everyone for 10 Minutes**. Afterwards, set it back to **Receiving Off**, because it goes back to **Contacts Only** on its own, not to off. The trade-off: one extra step each time you receive a file.
- **Turn off phone-to-phone sharing if you don't use it.** **Bringing Devices Together** (in **Settings > General > AirDrop**) lets you swap contact details or start AirDrop by holding two unlocked iPhones close. If you never do this, turn it off: one less thing that starts when phones touch. You can still share contacts the normal way.
- **Give your phone a name that doesn't say who you are.** Your phone's name can show up in AirDrop and Bluetooth lists, and it's also the Wi-Fi name of your **Personal Hotspot**. "Anna Smith's iPhone" tells a stranger your name. Change it in **Settings > General > About > Name** to something plain, like "Blue phone". The trade-off: friends may need a moment to spot you in their AirDrop list.

### 13. Check for unknown profiles (and install IT's profile, if they send one)

**Why this matters:** A "profile" is a small settings file that can change how your phone works. Some can route your internet through someone else's server. A scam site might trick you into installing one.

#### 13a. Check for anything unknown

1. Go to **Settings > General > VPN & Device Management**.
2. Look at what's listed under **Configuration Profile** or **VPN**.
3. If you see anything you didn't install and IT didn't send you, tap it and tap **Remove Profile**, enter your passcode, and confirm. If you're not sure, **don't remove it yet**. Take a screenshot and send it to IT.

**How to check it worked:** Only things you know about are listed, or the screen shows only **VPN: Not Connected** and nothing else.

#### 13b. Installing IT's safe-browsing profile (only if IT sends one)

IT may send you a profile that blocks dangerous websites. This is called DNS filtering. **IT will send you a link or a file**. Only install it if it came from IT directly, and you were expecting it.

1. Open what IT sent:
   - **If it's a link:** press and hold the link, then tap **Copy Link**. Open the **Safari** app (the blue compass), tap the address bar, tap **Paste and Go**. Use Safari even if you normally use another browser, because other browsers can't install profiles.
   - **If it's a file attached to an email in the Mail app:** just tap the attachment.
2. When asked "This website is trying to download a configuration profile," tap **Allow**, then **Close**.
3. Go to **Settings**. Near the top, tap **Profile Downloaded**. (If you don't see it, go to **Settings > General > VPN & Device Management** and tap the profile under **Downloaded Profile**.)
4. Check the name matches what IT told you. The screen shows a green **Verified** or a red **Not Verified** label. IT will tell you which one to expect. If the name or the label isn't what IT said, stop and ask IT. Never install a profile from anyone else, whatever the label says.
5. Tap **Install**, enter your passcode, then tap **Install** again, and **Done**.

**How to check it worked:** IT's profile shows in **Settings > General > VPN & Device Management**.

**Heads-up:** If you don't install a downloaded profile within 8 minutes, it disappears. Just open IT's link again.

**Going further:**
- **Check for trusted certificates.** A profile can add a "root certificate" that lets someone read your private web traffic. Go to **Settings > General > About** and scroll to the bottom to **Certificate Trust Settings**. If a certificate there is switched on and you don't know where it came from, take a screenshot and send it to IT before changing anything. On a work phone, IT's own certificate may be there on purpose.
- **Check your Wi-Fi's DNS setting.** DNS is like a phone book for websites, and a rigged one can send you to fake sites. Go to **Settings > Wi-Fi**, tap the **i** next to your network, and look at **Configure DNS**. It should normally say **Automatic**. If it says **Manual** and you didn't set it yourself, take a screenshot and ask IT before changing it.
- **Delete VPN apps you don't use.** A VPN app can see where your internet traffic goes. If **VPN & Device Management** lists a VPN from an app you no longer use, delete that app (see step 28). If you're not sure what it is, ask IT first.

### 14. Turn on Safari's fraud warning

**Why this matters:** Fake websites copy your bank or email login page to steal your password. Safari can warn you before the page opens.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Safari**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > Safari**.)
2. Scroll to **Privacy & Security**.
3. Turn on **Fraudulent Website Warning**.

**How to check it worked:** The switch is green.

**Going further:**
- **Check Safari's anti-tracking setting.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Safari > Advanced > Advanced Tracking and Fingerprinting Protection** and make sure **All Browsing** is chosen. On iOS 26 this is the normal setting, but check anyway, and change it if it says **Private Browsing** or **Off**. The trade-off: once in a while a website doesn't load properly. You can reload just that page with less protection from the page menu in Safari's address bar.
- **Let your password manager be your second fraud check.** The **Passwords** app only offers to fill in a password on the real website it was saved for. If it doesn't offer your bank password on a page that looks like your bank, stop: you may be on a fake copy. Also open **Passwords** and tap **Security** to see passwords that were leaked or used more than once, and change those.
- **Hide your internet address with iCloud+.** If you pay for iCloud+, go to **Settings > [your name] > iCloud > Private Relay** and turn it on. Websites you visit in Safari then can't see your real internet address (IP address), only a general area. The trade-off: some sites show the wrong region or ask you to prove you're human more often, some work or school networks block it, and it isn't offered in every country. If IT gave you a safe-browsing profile (step 13b), ask IT before turning this on, because Private Relay can make Safari skip that filter.

### 15. Turn on automatic app updates

**Why this matters:** Apps have security holes too. Updates fix them, but only if you install them.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > App Store**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > App Store**.)
2. Under **Automatic Downloads**, turn on **App Updates**.

**How to check it worked:** **App Updates** is green.

**Going further:**
- **Update by hand after big security news.** Automatic updates can take a few days to arrive. If you hear about a serious app problem, open the **App Store**, tap your picture in the top corner, pull the screen down to refresh, and tap **Update All**.
- **Check who made an app before you install it.** Fake apps copy the names and icons of real ones. On the App Store page, look at the company name under the app's title. It should be the real company, like your bank's name. Also check **Version History**: an app that hasn't been updated in years may no longer get security fixes.
- **Report scam apps and charges you didn't expect.** If an app tricked you or charged you for something you didn't agree to, go to **reportaproblem.apple.com**, sign in with your Apple Account, and report it. You may get a refund, and it helps Apple find bad apps. Deleting an app doesn't cancel its subscription, so also check **Settings > [your name] > Subscriptions** and cancel there. Then delete the app (see step 28).

### 16. Never "Trust" an unknown computer

**Why this matters:** When you plug your iPhone into a computer, a message asks **Trust This Computer?** If you tap **Trust**, that computer can copy your photos, messages and files. Someone else's laptop, or a public charging point with a computer hidden inside, could do this, but only if you tap **Trust** and type your passcode.

**What to do**

- Only tap **Trust** for your **own** computer.
- If you're just charging, at an airport, a hotel, or a friend's laptop, tap **Don't Trust**. It will still charge.
- Use your own charger and plug when you can.

**Going further:**
- **Clear out trusted computers if you need to.** Your iPhone forgets a trusted computer by itself if you don't connect to it for about a month. So you only need this if you tapped **Trust** on someone else's computer in the last few weeks. Go to **Settings > General > Transfer or Reset iPhone > Reset**, tap **Reset Location & Privacy**, enter your passcode and confirm. It doesn't delete your photos or apps. The trade-off: every app's permission to use your location, camera, microphone and so on is reset, so each app asks again the first time you open it. Say **No** to apps that don't need it. Afterwards, check that **Significant Locations** is still on (step 8), because Stolen Device Protection needs it. To check it worked: next time you plug into any computer, including your own, it asks **Trust This Computer?** again.
- **Use a charge-only cable or adapter away from home.** A small "data blocker" adapter, or a cable sold as charge-only, passes power but no data. Then a dodgy charging point can't talk to your phone at all, even if you tap the wrong button. The trade-off: it won't work for wired CarPlay or for backing up to your own computer, so keep it for travel.
- **Lock out Face ID before handing your phone over.** Use the side-button trick from step 5's Going further box, so the phone needs your passcode.
- **Restart your phone once a week.** Some rare, advanced attacks only live in the phone's memory and are wiped by a restart. Just after a restart, your phone is also at its most locked-down until you type your passcode. If you use a SIM PIN (step 11), you'll need to type that too.

### 17. Know what to do if your phone is lost

**Why this matters:** The first 30 minutes matter. Knowing these steps now means you won't panic later.

**Steps: use Mark As Lost**

1. On another device or a computer, go to **icloud.com/find** and sign in with your Apple Account. You won't need a code from your lost phone to use this page. (Or use the **Find My** app on another Apple device you own. On a friend's or family member's iPhone, open **Find My**, tap **Me**, then tap **Help a Friend** at the bottom.)
2. Choose your iPhone from the list of devices.
3. Tap **Mark As Lost** (on icloud.com it may be shown as **Lost Mode**). Turn it on.
4. Type a phone number where you can be reached and a short message, like "This phone is lost. Please call 555-0100." Tap **Activate** (or **Next** and then **Activate**).
5. If you think it's nearby, tap **Play Sound** first.

**What Mark As Lost does:** It locks the phone, shows your message on the screen, pauses Apple Pay and your cards, and keeps tracking its location.

**Then:**
- **Tell IT straight away**, even if it's your personal phone, because it has work data on it.
- Call your carrier. Ask them to suspend the line, then move your number to a new SIM or eSIM, so your sign-in codes can reach you again. (If you set a SIM PIN in step 11, it buys you time.)
- If it's stolen, report it to the police. Don't try to get it back yourself.
- Only use **Erase This Device** if you're sure it's gone for good. After erasing, you can no longer see where it is. Find My still stops anyone else using it.
- **Don't remove the phone from your account**, even after erasing it, and even if someone asks, begs or threatens you to. If you do, anyone can set it up again as their own.
- **Expect fake "we found your iPhone" messages.** Thieves often text or email you pretending to be Apple, sometimes weeks later. The message may name your phone's model and color, and has a link to sign in. It's a trick to get your Apple Account password so they can unlock the phone. Apple never texts or emails you to say a lost phone was found. Don't tap the link. To check on your phone, type **icloud.com/find** yourself.

**How to check it worked (practice now):** Go to **icloud.com/find** on a computer and check you can sign in and see your iPhone. Don't press Mark As Lost now.

**Going further:**
- **Make a "lost phone" card now.** On paper or in a password manager you can reach from another device, write down your carrier's phone number, IT's contact details, and your phone's **IMEI** number. You'll find the IMEI in **Settings > General > About**. Your carrier and the police may ask for it. Keep the card away from your phone.
- **Know your Apple Account password without your phone.** **icloud.com/find** asks for your Apple Account password, and on a stressful day you may not remember it. Make sure you can find it without your iPhone, for example in a password manager on your computer or in a sealed note at home.
- **If the thief may have seen your passcode, act faster.** From another Apple device you own, or **account.apple.com**, change your Apple Account password straight away, and call your bank to block your cards and bank apps. Stolen Device Protection (step 8) slows a thief down, but it doesn't protect apps that open with just your passcode, or accounts that send codes by text message.
- **Share your location with one trusted person.** Then they can see where your phone is if you can't get to a computer. Step 7's **Going further** shows how.

### 18. Run Safety Check

**Why this matters:** Over the years, you may have shared your location, photos or notes with people, or given apps access you've forgotten about. Safety Check walks you through all of it in one place.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Safety Check** (scroll down to find it).
2. Tap **Manage Sharing & Access**, then **Continue**.
3. Follow the screens. Check who you share with, which apps can see what, and your account security. Stop sharing anything you no longer want to share.

**How to check it worked:** Safety Check finishes and takes you back to its start screen.

**Heads-up:** If you stop sharing with someone, you may also lose access to things they shared with you, like a shared photo album. **Emergency Reset** (also on this screen) is for people in an unsafe situation. It stops all sharing at once. You don't need it for this guide.

**Going further:**
- **Check sharing in other apps too.** Safety Check can take away other apps' permissions, but it can't see who you share with inside those apps. Apps like Google Maps, WhatsApp, Snapchat or Life360 have their own location sharing. Open each one you use and look for a **location sharing** setting. Stop sharing with anyone you no longer want to.
- **Make sure tracker alerts are on.** Your iPhone can warn you if someone's AirTag or similar tracker is moving with you. Go to **Settings > Notifications > Tracking Notifications** and make sure **Allow Notifications** is on. Keep **Bluetooth** and **Location Services** on for these alerts to work.
- **Look at your Family Sharing group.** Go to **Settings > Family** (on older versions, **Settings > [your name] > Family Sharing**). Check that everyone in the group should be there. Tap each person to see what you share with them, such as your location or purchases. Remove anyone who no longer belongs, and turn off sharing you don't want. The trade-off: the other person may notice they've been removed, so if you feel unsafe, read Apple's **Personal Safety User Guide** first.

### 19. Review app permissions

**Why this matters:** Apps you installed years ago may still be able to see your location all the time, or use your microphone. If an app doesn't need it, take it away.

**Steps**

Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security**, then go through each of these:

1. **Location Services**: **Leave the main Location Services switch at the top ON.** Turning it off stops Find My from showing where your phone is, and breaks Stolen Device Protection. Also leave **Find My** and **System Services** alone. Then tap each app in the list. Choose **While Using the App** or **Never**. Keep **Always** only for apps that truly need it, like a car-sharing or a family-locating app.
2. **Microphone**: Turn off any app that doesn't need to hear you.
3. **Camera**: Turn off any app that doesn't need to take photos or video.
4. **Photos**: Tap each app. Choose **Limited Access** (you pick which photos it can see) or **None** instead of **Full Access**, unless it's a photo app you trust.
5. **Local Network**: Turn off apps that don't need to find gadgets in your home (printers, speakers, TVs). Most apps don't need this.

**How to check it worked:** Each list only shows apps you're happy with.

**Heads-up:** If an app stops working properly (for example, a map app can't find you), go back and turn its permission back on.

**Going further:**
- **Turn off precise location for most apps.** In **Location Services**, tap an app and turn off **Precise Location**. The app then only knows roughly where you are, like the part of town. Weather, news and shopping apps work fine this way. The trade-off: keep it on for maps, ride-sharing and delivery apps, which need your exact spot.
- **Check Bluetooth and Contacts too.** In **Settings > Privacy & Security**, also look at **Bluetooth** and **Contacts**. Bluetooth access can help apps work out where you are, for example from signals in shops. For Contacts, on iOS 18 or later choose **Limited Access** so an app only sees the people you pick, or turn it off.
- **Turn on the App Privacy Report.** Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > App Privacy Report** and tap **Turn On App Privacy Report**. After a few days, it shows how often each app used your location, camera, microphone and contacts, and which websites it talked to. Anything surprising is a good reason to take a permission away or delete the app.
- **Lock private apps with Face ID.** On iOS 18 or later, press and hold an app's icon and tap **Require Face ID**. Then even someone you hand your unlocked phone to can't open that app, like your banking or email app. **Hide and Require Face ID** also hides the app in a locked folder. The trade-off: hidden apps don't show their notifications.

### 20. Check which devices get your texts and calls

**Why this matters:** Your texts and calls can also go to your Mac or iPad. If an old device, or one someone else uses, is on the list, that person may see your messages and login codes.

**Steps: texts**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Messages > Text Message Forwarding**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > Messages**.)
2. Turn off any device you don't recognise or no longer use.

**Steps: calls**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Phone > Calls on Other Devices**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > Phone**.)
2. Turn off any device you don't recognise or no longer use. If you never answer calls on other devices, turn off **Allow Calls on Other Devices**.

**How to check it worked:** Only your own devices are switched on.

**If you don't see this:** If **Text Message Forwarding** isn't there, iMessage may be off, or no other Apple device is signed in to your iMessage. That's fine.

**Going further:**
- **Check the addresses your iMessage uses.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Messages > Send & Receive**. Every phone number and email listed should be yours. Do the same for calls in **Settings > Apps > FaceTime**, under **You can be reached by FaceTime at**. Untick any email address you don't recognise or no longer use.
- **Remember that Messages in iCloud syncs your history.** If **Messages in iCloud** is on, any device signed in to your Apple Account with Messages turned on can show your old texts, not just new ones. That's why the device list in step 6c matters so much. Remove anything there you don't use.
- **Check your Mac too.** On a Mac, open **Messages**, choose **Messages > Settings**, then click **iMessage**, and check the addresses there. A Mac that someone else at home also uses is a common way texts end up on the wrong screen.
- **Want a warning if a strange device joins your chats?** **Contact Key Verification** (step 32) can alert you to that. It's in the "Only if IT tells you to" part because it needs all your devices up to date and can sign old ones out of iMessage. If you think you're being targeted, ask IT about it rather than turning it on yourself.

---

### 21. Make sure call forwarding is off

**Why this matters:** Scammers sometimes switch on call forwarding so that your calls, including the "your login code is..." calls from banks, go to them instead.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Phone > Call Forwarding**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > Phone > Call Forwarding**.)
2. Make sure **Call Forwarding** is **off**, unless you turned it on yourself on purpose.

**How to check it worked:** The switch is grey.

**If you don't see this:** Some carriers hide this option. In that case, check call forwarding in your carrier's app or website, or ask them.

**Going further:**
- **Give your voicemail a strong PIN.** Some banks read your login code out in a phone call. If you don't answer, it goes to voicemail, and criminals sometimes try to break into voicemail to hear it. Open **Settings**, type "Voicemail Password" in the search box, or use your carrier's app. Don't use your passcode, SIM PIN or birthday. (The **Staying safe online guide, step 4** has more on this.)
- **Check the hidden kinds of forwarding.** The switch in Settings only covers "forward every call." Calls can also be forwarded only when you're busy, don't answer, or have no signal. On AT&T, T-Mobile and most networks outside the US, dial **\*#67#** (busy), **\*#61#** (no answer) and **\*#62#** (no signal) in the Phone app to see where each one goes. Your own carrier's voicemail number is normal. Any other number isn't. On Verizon, or if you're not sure, ask your carrier to check for you.
- **Never dial a code someone asks you to.** A common scam is a "delivery" or "support" caller asking you to dial a short code, such as one starting with **\*72** or **\*\*21\***. That code quietly forwards your calls to them. Hang up instead.
- **Turn on account alerts with your carrier.** In your carrier's app, turn on notifications for account changes, such as SIM changes, forwarding or number transfers, if they offer them. Then you hear about a takeover attempt straight away. This adds to the carrier lock in step 11b.

### 22. Screen messages from unknown senders

**Why this matters:** Most scam texts come from numbers you don't know. This puts them in a separate list, so you're less likely to tap a bad link.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Messages**.
2. Scroll to **Unknown Senders**. Turn on **Screen Unknown Senders**. (On iOS 18, this is called **Filter Unknown Senders**, under **Message Filtering**.)

**How to check it worked:** In the Messages app, tap the filter button in the top corner. You'll see an **Unknown Senders** list.

**Heads-up:** Texts from new numbers, like a delivery driver or a new doctor's office, go into that list. Login codes and urgent alerts usually still notify you. Whether other texts from new numbers notify you depends on your country. In some countries you can choose this in **Settings > Apps > Messages > Unknown Senders > Allow Notifications**. Check **Unknown Senders** now and then.

**Going further:**
- **Screen unknown calls too (iOS 26).** Go to **Settings > Apps > Phone**, and under **Screen Unknown Callers** choose **Ask Reason for Calling**. Your phone asks unknown callers who they are and why they're calling, then rings and shows their answer on screen. Trade-off: some genuine callers, like a doctor's office or a delivery driver, hang up instead of answering. If you don't see the option, your carrier, language or region may not support it yet.
- **Keep the spam filter on.** In **Settings > Apps > Messages**, look for **Filter Spam** (iOS 26) and check it's on. Texts your iPhone thinks are spam go to a **Spam** list and don't notify you. Glance at that list now and then in case something real lands there.
- **Report scam texts, don't just delete them.** Tap **Report Junk** under the message, or forward it to **7726**. See the **Staying safe online guide, step 26** for more.
- **Blur unwanted pictures.** Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Sensitive Content Warning** and turn it on. Nude images sent to you in Messages, AirDrop and FaceTime are blurred until you choose to view them. The check happens on your phone, so Apple doesn't see the images.

---

### 23. Review Safari extensions

**Why this matters:** Extensions are add-ons for Safari. A bad one can read every page you visit, including your bank.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Apps > Safari > Extensions**. (On iOS 17 and older: **Settings > Safari > Extensions**.)
2. Tap each extension. If you don't know what it is or don't use it, turn it off. Better still, delete the app that came with it.

**How to check it worked:** Only extensions you know and use are on.

**Going further:**
- **Limit what each extension can see.** In **Settings > Apps > Safari > Extensions**, tap an extension you keep. Set **Other Websites** to **Ask** or **Deny**, and allow only the sites it actually needs. Leave it off in **Private Browsing** unless you need it there.
- **Turn on fingerprinting protection everywhere.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Safari > Advanced > Advanced Tracking and Fingerprinting Protection** and choose **All Browsing**. iOS 26 protects all browsing by default, but older versions only protected Private Browsing, so check it. Trade-off: a few sites may load oddly. If that bothers you, set it back to **Private Browsing**.
- **Add one trusted content blocker.** A content blocker such as **AdGuard** or **1Blocker** from the App Store stops many ads and trackers, which is where many fake "virus" pop-ups come from. Both have a free version. A plain content blocker can't read your pages the way a normal extension can, but some of these apps offer extra add-ons that can, so leave those off. Add only one, and on a work phone, ask IT first. Some sites will complain or break, and you can switch the blocker off for those sites.
- **Lock Private Browsing with Face ID.** In **Settings > Apps > Safari**, check **Require Face ID to Unlock Private Browsing** is on (it says Touch ID or passcode on some phones). Then anyone you hand your unlocked phone to can't open your private tabs.

### 24. Tidy up Wi-Fi settings

**Why this matters:** Public Wi-Fi (coffee shops, hotels) is fine for everyday browsing, because almost all sites and apps encrypt what you send. The real risks are fake networks with a familiar name, which anyone can set up, and Wi-Fi sign-in pages that ask for more than they should. This step tidies up which networks your phone joins by itself.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Wi-Fi**.
2. Tap **Auto-Join Hotspot** and choose **Ask to Join** (or **Never**). This stops your phone quietly switching to your own or your family's iPhone or iPad hotspot (which can use up mobile data). It's more about tidiness than safety.
3. Tap **Edit** in the top corner to see your saved networks. Delete old public ones, like airports, hotels and cafes: tap the red minus, then **Delete**.
4. For each network you still use, tap the **i** next to it and check **Private Wi-Fi Address** is on. On iOS 18 and 26, choose **Fixed** or **Rotating**, not **Off**. (On older versions it's a simple on/off switch.)

**How to check it worked:** Your saved list only has networks you trust. Auto-Join Hotspot shows **Ask to Join** or **Never**.

**Heads-up:**
- Only delete networks you won't use again. You'll need the Wi-Fi password to rejoin, and the network may also disappear from your other Apple devices.
- If a Wi-Fi network at work or home stops working after you change **Private Wi-Fi Address**, set it to **Fixed** for that one network, or ask IT.

**Going further:**
- **Stop auto-joining networks you keep.** For public networks you still use, like your gym or a favourite cafe, tap the **i** next to the network and turn off **Auto-Join**. Your phone then joins only when you choose, so a fake network with the same name can't grab it as you walk past.
- **Use iCloud Private Relay on public Wi-Fi.** If you pay for iCloud+, go to **Settings > [your name] > iCloud > Private Relay** and turn it on. Your Safari browsing is then encrypted and your rough location is hidden from websites, even on a dodgy hotel network. Trade-off: some work networks, banks and streaming sites don't work with it, and it isn't offered in every country. You can turn it off for one network by tapping the **i** next to it and turning off **Limit IP Address Tracking**. If IT gave you a safe-browsing profile (step 13b), ask IT before turning this on, because Private Relay can make Safari skip that filter.
- **Use your own mobile data for anything important.** For banking or work email away from home, mobile data (or your own phone's **Personal Hotspot** for a laptop) is the simplest choice if you're unsure about a network. Trade-off: it uses your data allowance.
- **Be careful with free VPN apps.** Many free VPNs make money from your data. If you think you need one, especially for work, ask IT which one to use.

### 25. Review Shortcuts automations

**Why this matters:** The Shortcuts app can run actions on its own, like sending your location to someone. If someone set one up on your phone, you might never notice.

**Steps**

1. Open the **Shortcuts** app.
2. Tap **Automation** at the bottom.
3. Look at each one. If you didn't create it, or don't need it, swipe left on it and tap **Delete**.

**How to check it worked:** The list is empty, or only has automations you made yourself.

**If you don't see this:** If you deleted the Shortcuts app, there's nothing to check.

**Going further:**
- **Make automations ask before they run.** Tap each automation you keep and choose **Run After Confirmation** instead of **Run Immediately**. If you'd rather keep **Run Immediately**, at least turn on **Notify When Run**. Then nothing can happen quietly in the background. Trade-off: you'll need to tap a notification for some automations to run.
- **Keep the risky Shortcuts settings off.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Shortcuts > Advanced**. Leave **Allow Running Scripts** and **Allow Sharing Large Amounts of Data** off unless a shortcut you trust really needs them. Apple itself warns that shortcuts with scripts can cause data loss.
- **Only add shortcuts from people you trust.** A shared shortcut can send your location, photos or messages anywhere. Before tapping **Add Shortcut**, scroll through its actions and look for anything that sends, uploads or shares.
- **Check your Home app too.** If you use the **Home** app, open its **Automation** tab and remove anything you didn't set up, such as a lock that opens at a certain time.

### 26. Check accessibility settings for anything you didn't set up

**Why this matters:** Accessibility features are great for people who need them. But a few of them, if someone turns them on without you knowing, can be misused. For example, Live Listen can turn your phone into a microphone that someone listens to through AirPods. Only change the ones you **don't** use.

**Steps: Live Listen**

1. Open Control Center (swipe down from the top right corner).
2. If you see a **Hearing** button (an ear icon) and you don't use it, tap it and make sure **Live Listen** is off.
3. To remove it: tap the **+** in the top left corner, then tap the minus on the **Hearing** button, then tap an empty area to finish.

**Steps: other features to check**

Go to **Settings > Accessibility** and check each of these:

1. **Hearing Devices**: If you see a hearing aid you don't own, tap its name, then tap **Forget This Device**.
2. **Switch Control** (under **Physical and Motor**): Should be off unless you use it.
3. **Voice Control** (under **Physical and Motor**): Should be off unless you use it.
4. **Accessibility Shortcut** (at the very bottom): This is what happens when you triple-click the side button (or the Home button on phones that have one). Untick anything you don't use.

**About Control Nearby Devices:** This lets **your** iPhone act as a remote for your other Apple devices signed in to the same Apple Account. There's no switch to turn off. It only runs when you tap it. Just make sure it isn't ticked in your **Accessibility Shortcut** list if you don't use it.

Also go to **Settings > Bluetooth** and look at **My Devices**. If you see a headset, speaker or device you don't own, tap the **i** next to it, then **Forget This Device**.

**How to check it worked:** Triple-click the side button (or the Home button on phones that have one). Nothing happens, or only a feature you use comes on.

**Heads-up:** If you or someone in your family relies on any of these features, keep them on. This is only about features you didn't set up.

**Going further:**
- **Check Back Tap.** Go to **Settings > Accessibility > Touch > Back Tap**. **Double Tap** and **Triple Tap** should say **None**, or something you chose yourself. Back Tap can run any shortcut, so one you didn't set up could start just by tapping the back of the phone.
- **Check Vocal Shortcuts (iOS 18 and later).** Go to **Settings > Accessibility > Vocal Shortcuts**. If there's a phrase you didn't set up, delete it. A saved phrase can run an action or shortcut when it's spoken.
- **Use Guided Access when you hand your phone over.** Turn it on in **Settings > Accessibility > Guided Access**, and set its own passcode under **Passcode Settings**. Then open an app, like Photos, and triple-click the side button. The person holding the phone is stuck in that one app until you triple-click again and enter the code. Trade-off: if other features are also on your **Accessibility Shortcut** (see the main part of this step), a triple-click shows a short menu to choose from.

### 27. Cut down tracking and ad sharing

**Why this matters:** Apps and advertisers build a profile of what you do. Less sharing means less data about you if one of them is hacked.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Tracking**. Turn off **Allow Apps to Request to Track**.
2. Go back to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Analytics & Improvements**. Turn off **Share iPhone Analytics** (it may say **Share iPhone & Watch Analytics**), and anything else on that screen.
3. Go back to **Settings > Privacy & Security > Apple Advertising**. Turn off **Personalized Ads**.

**How to check it worked:** All three switches are grey.

**Heads-up:** You'll still see ads. They'll just be less personal.

**Going further:**
- **Hide when you open emails.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Mail > Privacy Protection** and turn on **Protect Mail Activity**. Senders can no longer see when you open an email or where you are. It only works in Apple's **Mail** app.
- **Hide your address from trackers in Safari.** Go to **Settings > Apps > Safari > Hide IP Address** and choose **Trackers Only**. It's free and stops known trackers seeing your rough location. With iCloud+, **Trackers and Websites** and **Private Relay** (see step 24) go further.
- **Turn off Precise Location for apps that don't need it.** In **Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services**, tap an app like weather or shopping, and turn off **Precise Location**. It then only knows your rough area. Keep it on for maps and ride apps, and leave **Find My** and **System Services** alone (step 19).
- **See what your apps really do.** Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security > App Privacy Report** and turn it on. After a week, it shows which apps used your location, camera or microphone, and which websites they contacted. Delete or limit any app that surprises you (steps 19 and 28).

### 28. Delete apps you don't use

**Why this matters:** Every app is another door. Old apps stop getting fixes, and some still collect data in the background.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > General > iPhone Storage**. Wait for the list to load. It shows when you last used each app.
2. Tap an app you don't need, then tap **Delete App** and confirm.
3. Or on the Home Screen: press and hold the app, tap **Remove App**, then **Delete App**.

**How to check it worked:** The app is gone from your Home Screen and App Library.

**Heads-up:** Deleting an app may delete what's saved inside it. If you want to keep the data but free up space, choose **Offload App** instead. But for security, deleting is better.

**Going further:**
- **Cancel subscriptions before you delete.** Deleting an app doesn't stop its payments. Go to **Settings > [your name] > Subscriptions** and cancel anything you don't use. If an app charged you for something you didn't agree to, report it at **reportaproblem.apple.com**.
- **Delete your account inside the app first.** For apps that have an account (shopping, social, fitness), look in the app's settings for **Delete Account** before removing the app. Otherwise your data stays on the company's servers. If you used **Sign in with Apple** for it, go to **Settings > [your name]** and find **Sign in with Apple** (on some versions it's under **Sign-In & Security**). Tap the app and choose to stop using Sign in with Apple. This only unlinks it, so delete the account in the app first.
- **Lock private apps you keep (iOS 18 and later).** Press and hold an app such as banking, Photos or Notes, tap **Require Face ID** (or Touch ID or passcode), and confirm. Then anyone using your unlocked phone can't open that app without your face or passcode. **Hide and Require Face ID** also moves it to a locked **Hidden** folder in the App Library. Trade-off: not every app can be hidden, and hidden apps don't send you notifications or calls.

## Part 3: Only if IT tells you to

These are strong protections for people who might be targeted on purpose, for example people who handle very sensitive information or travel to high-risk places. They have real downsides. **Don't turn them on unless IT has asked you to.**

**One exception:** security keys (step 31) on a personal Apple Account. If you're comfortable with tech, you can add them yourself after reading the downsides. If you use that Apple Account on any device you use for work, ask IT first.

### 29. Lockdown Mode

**What it is:** An extreme protection mode. It switches off many features that attackers use to break into phones.

**Before you start:** If IT sent you a profile (step 13b), install it **first**. You can't install profiles while Lockdown Mode is on.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > Privacy & Security**, scroll down and tap **Lockdown Mode**.
2. Tap **Turn On Lockdown Mode**.
3. Tap **Turn On Lockdown Mode** again to confirm.
4. Tap **Turn On & Restart**, and enter your passcode.

**How to check it worked:** **Settings > Privacy & Security > Lockdown Mode** shows **On**. Safari shows a banner saying Lockdown Mode is on.

**Downsides:**
- You can't install new profiles, including IT's safe-browsing profile. If you need one later, turn Lockdown Mode off, install it, then turn Lockdown Mode back on.
- Most message attachments are blocked (some images, video and audio still work). Links and link previews in Messages don't work.
- Some websites look broken or load slowly.
- FaceTime calls are blocked from people you haven't called in the past 30 days.
- Shared albums are removed.
- Your phone must be unlocked to connect to accessories or computers.
- You can turn off Lockdown Mode for specific websites or apps if needed.

---

### 30. Advanced Data Protection

**What it is:** Extra encryption for almost everything in iCloud, including backups, photos and notes. Even Apple can't read it.

**Steps**

1. First make sure you have a **Recovery Contact** (step 6d). Apple also offers a **Recovery Key** (a long code you must store yourself). Only set up a Recovery Key if IT walks you through it, because losing it can lock you out for good.
2. Go to **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**.
3. Scroll down and tap **Advanced Data Protection**.
4. Tap **Turn On Advanced Data Protection** and follow the prompts.

**How to check it worked:** **Advanced Data Protection** shows **On**.

**Downsides:** If you forget your password and lose your Recovery Contact or Recovery Key, **Apple cannot get your data back. It's gone for good.** You can't view your data on icloud.com by default. All your Apple devices must be up to date. With Stolen Device Protection set to **Always** (step 8), turning this on may make you wait an hour.

**If you don't see this:** Advanced Data Protection is not available in every country (for example, Apple withdrew it in the UK).

---

### 31. Security keys

**What it is:** A small physical key (like a USB stick) that you use instead of a code when signing in to your Apple Account on a new device. It's very hard to phish.

**Steps**

1. You need **two** security keys that work with Apple. For a work account, IT will usually give these to you.
2. Go to **Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Two-Factor Authentication > Security Keys**.
3. Tap **Add Security Keys** and follow the prompts to add both keys.
4. At the end, you'll see a list of devices signed in to your account. Keep the ones you use.

**How to check it worked:** Both keys show in the Security Keys list.

**Downsides:**
- If you lose **both** keys and all your Apple devices, you may be locked out of your Apple Account for good, and Apple can't help. Keep the second key somewhere safe at home.
- Every device signed in to your account needs iOS 16.3, iPadOS 16.3 or macOS 13.2 or later. Older devices can't stay signed in.
- Devices you haven't used in more than 90 days get signed out when you set this up.
- On a Windows PC, iCloud for Windows must be version 15 or later.
- It doesn't work with child accounts or company-managed (Managed) Apple Accounts.
- With Stolen Device Protection set to **Always** (step 8), adding keys may make you wait an hour.

---

### 32. Contact Key Verification

**What it is:** A way to make sure you're messaging the real person in iMessage, not an impostor. If someone sneaks a fake device into a conversation, you get a warning.

**Steps**

1. Go to **Settings > [your name] > Contact Key Verification**.
2. Turn on **Verification in iMessage**, then tap **Continue**.
3. Follow the prompts. You may need to update or sign out of iMessage on old devices.

**How to check it worked:** **Verification in iMessage** is green. In a chat with someone who also has it on, you can compare verification codes in person.

**Downsides:** All your Apple devices signed in to iMessage need to be up to date, and old ones may need to sign out of iMessage. It doesn't work with company-managed (Managed) Apple Accounts. It only helps if the other person turns it on too.

Great work. Your iPhone is now much harder to break into, and much easier to get back if it's lost.

**Every few months**, take 5 minutes to:
- Check **Settings > General > Software Update**.
- Look at the device list in **Settings > [your name]**.
- Run **Safety Check** again.

## Securing Your Mac: A Step-by-Step Guide

### What this is

Nobody has switched on this Mac's security settings for you. On a company-managed Mac, IT does that remotely. On yours, you'll do it yourself with this guide, one setting at a time.

You don't need to be "good with computers." Every step tells you exactly what to click. Words in **bold** are the exact words you'll see on screen. A path like **System Settings > General > Software Update** means: open System Settings, click **General**, then click **Software Update**.

This guide is written for **macOS 26 Tahoe**. If your Mac is still on **macOS 15 Sequoia**, it mostly looks the same. Where something has a different name, we say so.

Some steps end with a **Going further** box. It stays closed until you click it, and holds extra protection for people who are comfortable with tech. Everyone else can skip these boxes: the main steps already protect you well.

### How long it takes

- **Must-do section:** about 60 to 75 minutes, plus time for the update to install and the first backup to run.
- **Nice-to-have section:** about 25 minutes.
- You don't have to do it all in one go. The checklist below helps you keep your place.

### What to have ready

- **Your Mac login password** (the one you type to unlock your Mac).
- **Your Apple Account password** (what Apple used to call your Apple ID). You'll need it for Find My Mac and some of the later steps.
- **Your charger.** Plug your Mac in. Updates and encryption won't run properly on battery.
- **A safe place to save a recovery key**, such as a password manager, or paper you can lock away. You'll need this in step 2.
- **An external drive** for backups (step 19). If you have one, **do step 19 first**, before step 0. No drive yet? Carry on, and do step 19 as soon as you have one.
- **The link or file from IT** for the web filtering profile (step 20), if IT has sent you one.

> **Note:** The carrier-lock step in the iPhone guide (step 11) needs your phone carrier login. You don't need it for the Mac.

### Before you start

#### 0. Update your Mac

**Why this matters:** Most attacks use security holes that Apple has already fixed. Updating closes those holes. Many later steps also assume you're on the latest version.

**Steps**
1. Save your work and plug in your charger.
2. Open **System Settings > General > Software Update**.
3. Wait while it checks. If an update appears, click **Update Now** (or **Restart Now** if it's already downloaded).
4. Enter your password if asked. Your Mac will restart, maybe more than once. This can take 20 to 60 minutes.
5. When it's back, open **Software Update** again and repeat until it says your Mac is up to date.

**How to check it worked:** **Software Update** says your Mac is up to date. On Sequoia: if the only thing left is an offer to upgrade to macOS Tahoe (usually shown as **Upgrade Now**), you're done with this step.

**If you don't see this:** If it offers an upgrade to a whole new macOS (for example, from Sequoia to Tahoe), check with IT before installing it. Some older Macs can't run macOS 26. That's OK for now. Install the latest updates for the version you have, and tell IT your Mac model.

**Going further:**
- **Only update from Apple's own screens.** Real macOS updates come from **System Settings > General > Software Update** or the **App Store**. A website pop-up saying your Mac "needs an update" or is "infected" is fake. Close the tab, and never download the "update" it offers.
- **Install urgent fixes the same day.** Apple lists its security fixes at **support.apple.com/100100**. When the notes for an update say Apple is aware the problem "may have been exploited", attackers are already using it. Install that update today instead of waiting for the weekend.
- **Know when your Mac stops getting fixes.** Apple gives the full set of security fixes only to the newest macOS. Older versions get some fixes for a while, then none. If your Mac can't move to macOS 26, ask IT when it should be replaced, so you're not left on a Mac that no longer gets fixes.

---

### Must-do (everyone)

#### 1. Turn on automatic updates (macOS and App Store)

**Why this matters:** You'll get urgent security fixes even if you forget to check. Some fixes install quietly in the background in a few minutes.

**Part A: macOS updates**
1. Open **System Settings > General > Software Update**.
2. Click the small **i** (info) button next to **Automatic Updates**.
3. Turn on all of these:
   - **Download new updates when available**
   - **Install macOS updates**
   - **Install Security Responses and system files** (small, urgent security fixes that install quickly, often without a restart)
4. Click **Done**.

**Part B: App updates from the App Store**
1. Open the **App Store** app.
2. In the menu bar click **App Store > Settings**.
3. Tick **Automatic Updates**.

**Part C: Background Security Improvements (macOS 26.1 and later)**
1. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Scroll down to **Background Security Improvements** and make sure **Automatically Install** is on. This lets Apple send small, urgent security fixes between normal updates. If it's off, you only get those fixes with the next full update.

**How to check it worked:** Click the **i** next to **Automatic Updates** again and confirm all three switches are on. Then reopen **App Store > Settings** and confirm **Automatic Updates** is still ticked. On macOS 26.1 or later, also check that **Automatically Install** is on under **Background Security Improvements**.

**If you don't see this:** On macOS 15 Sequoia, the **i** window in Part A also has **Check for updates** and **Install application updates from the App Store**. Turn those on too (the second one does the same job as Part B). If there's no **Background Security Improvements** in **Privacy & Security**, your Mac is on macOS 26.0 or older. Install any update step 0 offers, then look again. On macOS 15 Sequoia, skip Part C.

**Heads-up:** Your Mac may restart overnight to finish an update. Save your work before you leave it. Laptops only download updates when plugged in. Apps you installed from websites (not the App Store) update separately: accept their update prompts, or look in their menu for "Check for Updates."

**Going further:**
- **Give apps from websites a monthly check.** Browsers and apps you downloaded yourself update separately. Once a month, open each one's **About** or **Check for Updates** menu (in Chrome: **three dots > Help > About Google Chrome**) and install what it finds. Then quit and reopen the app: most updates only take effect after a restart.
- **Remove apps you no longer use.** An app you never open still needs updates, and abandoned apps stop getting them. Drag unused apps from **Applications** to the Trash. The trade-off: you'll need to download one again if you change your mind. On a work Mac, ask IT before removing anything you didn't install yourself.

#### 2. Turn on FileVault (disk encryption) and save your recovery key

**Why this matters:** If your Mac is lost or stolen, FileVault scrambles everything on it so no one can read your files without your password. Without it, someone who steals your Mac has a much easier time getting at your files.

**What's a recovery key?** It's a long code of letters and numbers. It's your spare key. If you ever forget your Mac password, the recovery key (or your Apple Account, if you chose that option) gets you back in. **If you lose your password and every way back in, your files are gone for good.** Not even Apple can get them back.

**Before you start:** If you have a backup drive, do step 19 (backups) before this step. Then, if anything goes wrong with your password or recovery key, your files are safe on the drive.

**Steps**
1. Plug in your charger.
2. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**. Scroll down to the **Security** section and click **FileVault**.
3. If it says FileVault is already on, skip to "How to check it worked."
4. Click **Turn On**. Enter your password if asked.
5. You'll be asked how you want to be able to unlock your disk if you forget your password. What you see depends on your exact version of macOS:
   - **On newer versions of macOS 26:** your Mac creates a recovery key and saves it in the **Passwords** app through iCloud Keychain (Apple's way of syncing your passwords between your Apple devices). It may also show you the key in a window called **Write Down Your Recovery Key**. Write it down or save it somewhere else as well (see below), then click **Done**.
   - **On macOS 15 Sequoia or early macOS 26:** you'll see two choices:
     - **Allow my iCloud account to unlock my disk**: easiest. Your Apple Account can unlock your Mac if you forget your password.
     - **Create a recovery key and do not use my iCloud account**: your Mac shows you a code. You are fully responsible for keeping it safe.
     - Either is fine. If you're not sure, pick the iCloud option, *and* write down a recovery key if one is shown.
6. Click **Continue**. If you see **Enable Users**, every person who logs in to this Mac needs to type their password here.
7. Encryption runs in the background. You can keep using your Mac. Keep it plugged in until it finishes.

**Where to keep your recovery key**
- In a password manager (the company one if you have it), saved as "Mac recovery key" with your Mac's name.
- Or on paper, stored somewhere locked and away from the Mac. Not on a sticky note on the laptop.
- Never only on the Mac itself. If you're locked out, you can't open a file that's on the locked Mac.
- Ask IT whether they want a copy. Some companies keep one for you.

**How to check it worked:**
- **System Settings > Privacy & Security > FileVault** shows that FileVault is turned on (or "encrypting," which is fine).
- **Important:** make sure you can see your recovery key from somewhere *other than this Mac*. For example: the **Passwords** app on your iPhone (search for "recovery key"), your password manager, or your paper copy. If the only copy is on this Mac, write it down now.

**Heads-up:** Your Mac will no longer log you in automatically. You'll always need your password at startup. That's a good thing.

**Going further:**
- **Check that your recovery key really works.** This only applies if your Mac gave you a recovery key. If you chose the iCloud option and never saw a key, skip this. Open **Terminal** (in **Applications > Utilities**), type `sudo fdesetup validaterecovery` yourself, exactly as shown here, and press Return. Type your Mac password (nothing shows as you type), then your recovery key with its dashes. **true** means the key is correct. If it says **false**, don't turn FileVault off: contact IT. Check again if your Mac ever gives you a new key. When you're done, close Terminal. Don't type anything else there.
- **Shut down, don't just close the lid, when the Mac is at risk.** While your Mac is asleep, the disk is still unlocked inside it. When it's fully shut down, FileVault locks everything until someone types the password. Shut down before you leave it in a car or hotel room, or carry it through airport checks. The trade-off: it takes a little longer to start.
- **Don't look for a firmware password on newer Macs.** Macs with Apple chips (M1 and later) don't have one. The closest thing, **Recovery Lock**, can only be set by IT's device management. For you, FileVault plus Find My Mac (step 14) is the protection. Older Intel Macs do have a firmware password, but forgetting it can mean a trip to Apple with proof of purchase, so ask IT before setting one.

#### 3. Turn on the firewall and stealth mode

**Why this matters:** On public Wi-Fi (cafés, hotels, airports) other people's devices can try to connect to your Mac. The firewall blocks connections you didn't ask for. Stealth mode makes your Mac harder to find on the network in the first place.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Network**, then click **Firewall**.
2. Turn on **Firewall**. Enter your password if asked.
3. Click **Options**.
4. Turn on **Enable stealth mode**.
5. Leave **Automatically allow built-in software to receive incoming connections** and **Automatically allow downloaded signed software to receive incoming connections** turned on. They let normal apps work without endless pop-ups.
6. Click **OK**.

**How to check it worked:** **Network > Firewall** shows the firewall as on, and **Enable stealth mode** stays on when you reopen **Options**.

**Heads-up:** Now and then an app may ask "Do you want the application to accept incoming network connections?" Click **Allow** only for apps you know and use (for example, a video-call app). If you're not sure, click **Deny**. You can change it later in **Options**.

**Going further:**
- **Block everything coming in on risky Wi-Fi.** In **System Settings > Network > Firewall > Options**, turn on **Block all incoming connections**. Browsing, email and most video calls still work. The trade-off: Screen Sharing, File Sharing and some apps that other devices connect to stop working, so you may want to turn it off again at home.
- **Tidy the allowed list.** The same **Options** window lists apps you've allowed to accept connections. Select any you no longer use and click the **minus (–)** button. If one is needed, the Mac will simply ask again. Ask IT about names you don't recognise before removing them.
- **Watch what goes out, too.** The built-in firewall only checks connections coming in. A free tool called **LuLu** (from Objective-See, at objective-see.org) asks you each time an app tries to connect out, which can catch software quietly sending your data away. **Little Snitch** does the same and costs money. The trade-off: lots of questions in the first few days, and blocking the wrong thing can break updates. On a work Mac, ask IT first.

#### 4. Lock the screen quickly and use a strong password

**Why this matters:** If you step away for coffee, anyone walking past can use an unlocked Mac, including your email. A short lock timer and a strong password stop that.

**Part A: Turn the screen off after 10 minutes**
1. Open **System Settings > Lock Screen**.
2. Set **Turn display off on battery when inactive** to **For 10 minutes** or less.
3. Set **Turn display off on power adapter when inactive** to **For 10 minutes** or less.

**Part B: Ask for the password straight away**
1. Still in **Lock Screen**, find **Require password after screen saver begins or display is turned off**.
2. Choose **Immediately**.

**Part C: Screen saver (optional)**
Parts A and B already lock your Mac. A screen saver is just extra. If you use one, make it start at the same time as the display turns off, or sooner.
- **On macOS 26 Tahoe:** open **System Settings > Wallpaper**, then click **Screen Saver** (you may need to scroll down). Choose a screen saver, and set **Start Screen Saver** to 10 minutes or less.
- **On macOS 15 Sequoia:** set **Start Screen Saver when inactive** in **System Settings > Lock Screen** (the same page as Parts A and B). The separate **Screen Saver** item only picks which screen saver to show.

**Part D: Use a strong login password**
A good password is long. Think of a short sentence you'll remember, like four or five random words. Don't use our example, but it looks like "violin cloudy parcel tractor." Avoid your name, birthday, pet's name or "Password1."
1. Open **System Settings > Users & Groups**.
2. Click the **i** button next to your name, then click **Change**.
3. Type your current password in **Old Password**, the new one in **New Password** and **Verify**, and add a hint that only you would understand. Click **Change Password**.
4. Save the new password in your password manager. If you ever forget it, your recovery key from step 2 gets you back in.

**Quick lock tip:** Press **Control + Command + Q** to lock your screen instantly when you walk away.

**How to check it worked:** Wait for the screen to go dark, then wake it. It should ask for your password right away.

**If you don't see this:** If the **Require password** menu is greyed out and shows **Immediately**, that's fine. iPhone Mirroring keeps it there. Nothing to change.

**Heads-up:**
- You'll type your password (or use Touch ID) more often. Touch ID makes this painless.
- After changing your password, your Mac may ask you to update your "keychain" password (the store where your Mac keeps saved passwords). Choose to update it and type your **old** password when asked.

**Going further:**
- **Lock by moving the pointer into a corner.** Open **System Settings > Desktop & Dock**, scroll down and click **Hot Corners**. Pick a corner and choose **Lock Screen**. To avoid locking by accident, hold **Command** (or another key) while you choose it. Then the corner only works while you hold that key down.
- **Use a login password you use nowhere else.** Your Mac password should be different from your Apple Account password and from every website password. If one of them leaks, the others stay safe. Your password manager can remember them for you.
- **Shorten the timer.** In **System Settings > Lock Screen**, set both **Turn display off** options to **For 2 minutes** or **For 5 minutes** instead of 10. A shorter gap gives someone less time at an unlocked Mac. The trade-off: the screen goes dark while you're reading, so you'll unlock more often. Touch ID makes that quick.

#### 5. Turn off the guest account and automatic login

**Why this matters:** A guest account lets anyone use your Mac without a password. Automatic login means anyone who turns on your Mac gets straight into your account.

**Part A: Turn off the guest account**
1. Open **System Settings > Users & Groups**.
2. Click the **i** button next to **Guest User**.
3. Turn off **Allow guests to log in to this computer**.
4. Click **OK**.

**Part B: Turn off automatic login**
1. Still in **Users & Groups**, find **Automatically log in as**.
2. Choose **Off**.

**How to check it worked:** Restart your Mac. You should see the login screen with no **Guest User** option.

**If you don't see this:** If **Automatically log in as** is missing or greyed out, FileVault is already on, which turns off automatic login for you. That's fine.

**Heads-up:** If you sometimes lend your Mac to someone, they'll need their own account, or you can log in for them while you're there.

**Going further:**
- **Make the login screen ask for a name too.** In **System Settings > Lock Screen**, set **Login window shows** to **Name and password**. Someone at your Mac then has to guess your account name as well as your password. The trade-off: you type your account name when switching users. The screen right after a restart may still show your name, because of FileVault.
- **Turn off password hints.** In the same place, turn off **Show password hints**. A hint can help someone who knows you guess your password. The hint you added in step 4 then won't show, so make sure the password is saved in your password manager first.
- **Lend your Mac with its own account.** If someone uses your Mac often, give them their own **Standard** account in **System Settings > Users & Groups > Add User**, not access to yours. The trade-off: every account added this way can also unlock the Mac at startup, so only add people you trust, and remove the account when they no longer need it.

#### 6. Check for unknown accounts

**Why this matters:** An extra account you didn't create, especially an "Admin" one, could let someone else control your Mac. It's rare, but worth a 30-second look.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Users & Groups**.
2. Look at the list of users. Each one shows **Admin** or **Standard** under its name.
3. Ask yourself: do I know every account here?
4. If you see one you don't recognise, **don't delete it yourself.** Write down its name and contact IT. Some are created by IT or by apps on purpose.
5. Now check your Apple Account. In **System Settings**, click **your name** at the top of the sidebar, then click **Sign-In & Security**. (Not signed in with an Apple Account? Skip this and the next item.)
6. Make sure **Two-Factor Authentication** says **On**, and that every phone number listed is yours or belongs to someone you really trust. If a number isn't, contact IT, or remove it from your iPhone (**iPhone guide, step 6b**). If you have an iPhone, the **iPhone guide, step 6** covers your Apple Account in full.

**How to check it worked:** Every account is one you (or IT) set up. Two-factor authentication is on, and every trusted phone number is one you know.

**Heads-up:** Deleting an account can delete its files. Leave this to IT if you're unsure.

**Going further:**
- **See accounts the settings page doesn't show.** Open **Terminal**, type `dscl . list /Users | grep -v '^_'` and press Return. You'll see **daemon**, **nobody** and **root**, which every Mac has, plus your own accounts. Anything else you don't recognise: take a screenshot and ask IT. Don't delete it yourself.
- **Check who has admin rights.** In Terminal, type `dscl . -read /Groups/admin GroupMembership` and press Return. It should list **root** and only the admin accounts you know about. An unknown name here is worth a call to IT straight away. Type these commands exactly as shown here, then close Terminal. Don't type anything else there, and never paste a command someone else sends you.
- **Check the devices on your Apple Account.** Open **System Settings**, click **your name**, and scroll down to the list of devices. If you see one that isn't yours, click it and choose **Remove from Account**, then change your Apple Account password.

#### 7. Turn off sharing services you don't use

**Why this matters:** Each sharing service opens a door so other computers can reach your Mac. Leaving doors open that you never use just gives attackers more chances.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > General > Sharing**.
2. Turn **off** each of these unless you know you use it:
   - **Screen Sharing** (others can see and control your screen)
   - **File Sharing** (others can open your shared folders)
   - **Media Sharing** (shares your music and videos on the network)
   - **Printer Sharing** (shares a printer connected to your Mac)
   - **Remote Login** (lets someone log in from another computer)
   - **Remote Management** (lets someone control your Mac with Apple Remote Desktop)
   - **Remote Application Scripting** (lets other Macs control apps on yours)
   - **Internet Sharing** (turns your Mac into a Wi-Fi hotspot)
   - **Bluetooth Sharing** (lets nearby devices send you files over Bluetooth)
   - **Content Caching** (stores Apple downloads to share with other devices)
3. You might not see every item on every Mac. That's normal.

**How to check it worked:** Every switch in **Sharing** is off, except anything you deliberately use.

**Heads-up:** If you share a printer at home or use a remote-support tool, that feature will stop until you turn its switch back on. If IT uses remote support with you, ask them which (if any) to leave on.

**Going further:**
- **Give your Mac a name that doesn't say who you are.** A name like "Jane's MacBook Pro" is visible to others on café and hotel Wi-Fi. Change it in **System Settings > General > About > Name**. Then, in **General > Sharing**, check **Local hostname** at the bottom. If it still shows your name, click **Edit** and change it too. The trade-off: it may be harder to spot in a list. On a work Mac, ask IT first, as they may use the name to find it.
- **If you need a sharing service, narrow it.** Click the **i** next to that service (for example **Screen Sharing** or **Remote Login**). Where you see **Allow access for**, choose **Only these users** and add just yourself. Turn the service off again when you're done.
- **Look again after installing support tools.** Remote-support and file-sync apps sometimes switch sharing services on during setup. After installing one, reopen **General > Sharing** and check nothing has turned on that you didn't expect.

#### 8. Turn off AirPlay Receiver and set AirDrop to Contacts Only

**Why this matters:** AirPlay Receiver lets nearby devices send video and sound to your Mac. AirDrop set to "Everyone" lets strangers on a train send you pictures or files out of the blue.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > General > AirDrop & Continuity**. On macOS 15 Sequoia this is called **AirDrop & Handoff**.
2. Turn off **AirPlay Receiver**.
3. Next to **AirDrop**, choose **Contacts Only**.

**How to check it worked:** Reopen the page. **AirPlay Receiver** is off and **AirDrop** shows **Contacts Only**.

**If you don't see this:** You can also set AirDrop from **Control Center** (the two-switches icon in the top-right of your screen): click **AirDrop**, then **Contacts Only**.

**Heads-up:** You won't be able to show your iPhone screen on your Mac with AirPlay until you turn it back on. If a colleague isn't in your Contacts, they'll need to be added before they can AirDrop you something (on macOS 26.2 and later they can also use an AirDrop code).

**Going further:**
- **If you turn AirPlay Receiver back on, lock it down.** In **System Settings > General > AirDrop & Continuity**, set **Allow AirPlay for** to **Current User**. Then only devices signed in to your own Apple Account can use it. Also turn on **Require password** and set one. Turn it off again when you're done. The trade-off: guests can't show their screen on your Mac.
- **Turn AirDrop off when you're not using it.** Next to **AirDrop**, choose **No One**. Nobody can send you anything, not even contacts. Switch it back to **Contacts Only** when you want to receive something. The trade-off: one extra step each time.

#### 9. Only allow apps from the App Store and known developers

**Why this matters:** This stops random, unchecked apps from running. It's one of the main things that protects Macs from fake "video player" or "cleaner" apps that are really malware.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Scroll down to **Security**.
3. Next to **Allow applications from**, choose **App Store & Known Developers**.

**How to check it worked:** It shows **App Store & Known Developers** (or the stricter **App Store**, which is also fine).

**Heads-up:** If you ever see a warning that an app "cannot be opened" or "could not be verified," stop. Don't look up ways around it. Ask IT first.

**Going further:**
- **Check who made an app before you trust it.** Open **Terminal**, type `spctl -a -vv ` (with a space at the end), drag the app from **Applications** into the Terminal window, and press Return. A safe app says **accepted**, and the **origin** line names the company you expected (App Store apps show Apple). If it says **rejected**, or names someone else, don't open it. Ask IT. Then close Terminal. Don't type anything else there.
- **Go stricter with "App Store" only.** Choosing **App Store** blocks every new app from a website. The trade-off: to install something like Chrome or Zoom later, you'll have to switch back to **App Store & Known Developers**, and it's easy to forget to switch again afterwards.
- **Never paste commands a website or installer gives you.** A common trick is a page that says "to fix this" or "to verify you're human", copy this line into **Terminal**. That line installs malware and skips every check in this step. No real website needs you to do that. Close the page and ask IT. Only type Terminal commands from this guide or from IT.

#### 10. Require an admin password for system settings

**Why this matters:** This makes sure important settings (like the ones in this guide) can't be changed without an administrator password. It's extra protection if someone else uses your Mac.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Scroll to the bottom and click **Advanced**.
3. Turn on **Require an administrator password to access system-wide settings**.
4. Click **Done**.

**How to check it worked:** Reopen **Advanced**. The switch stays on.

**Heads-up:** You'll be asked for your password a bit more often when changing settings. In the same window, you'll also see **Log out automatically after inactivity**. Leave it off unless IT asks; it can close unsaved work.

**Going further:**
- **Make this password different from your daily one.** If your everyday account is an admin, this step asks for your own password, which someone who watched you type it already knows. Step 21 sets up a separate admin account so this lock really needs a second password. The trade-off: you type that admin password a little more often.
- **Approve new USB devices (Mac laptops with Apple chips).** In **System Settings > Privacy & Security**, check that **Allow accessories to connect** says **Ask for New Accessories**, not **Always Allow** (called **Always** on some versions). Then an unknown cable or USB stick can't swap data with your Mac until you click **Allow**. It can still charge. The trade-off: you approve each new dock or drive once. Desktop Macs don't have this setting.
- **If you think someone is targeting you personally,** read step 23 (Lockdown Mode) and talk to IT. It goes much further than the settings here.

#### 11. Review which apps can see your screen, keyboard, files, camera and microphone

**Why this matters:** Some permissions are very powerful. An app with "Accessibility" or "Input Monitoring" access could read what you type, including passwords. Make sure only apps you trust have them.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Click each of these one at a time:
   - **Accessibility** (can control your Mac)
   - **Input Monitoring** (can see what you type)
   - **Screen & System Audio Recording** (can see your screen and hear system sound). On older versions this is called **Screen Recording**.
   - **Full Disk Access** (can read all your files)
   - **Microphone**
   - **Camera**
   - **Automation** (apps that can control other apps)
3. In each list, look at every app. Ask: do I recognise this app, and do I use it for something that needs this?
4. **Apps you know but no longer use:** turn their switch **off**. If you're sure you'll never need them, you can select the app and click the **minus (–)** button below the list.
5. **Names you don't recognise:** **don't switch them off yourself.** Take a screenshot and ask IT first. This matters most for anything that looks like a security, antivirus, VPN, remote-support or management tool, or an odd system-sounding name. Switching those off can quietly break your protection or IT's ability to help you.
6. For **Automation**, click the arrow next to an app to see what it can control. Turn off anything you know you don't need; ask IT about anything you don't recognise.

**What's normal:** video-call apps (Zoom, Teams, Meet) under Camera, Microphone and Screen Recording; password managers or accessibility tools under Accessibility; backup or security apps under Full Disk Access.

**How to check it worked:** Every app in these lists is one you know and use, or one IT has told you is fine.

**Heads-up:** If you turn off something you actually need, the app will stop working properly or ask you again. Just turn it back on.

**Going further:**
- **Watch the menu bar.** An **orange dot** means an app is using the microphone. The camera's **green light** means it's on. A **purple icon** means something is recording the screen. Click **Control Center** to see which app it is. If you didn't expect it, quit that app and check its permissions here.
- **Check the quieter lists too.** In **Privacy & Security**, also look at **Files & Folders**, **App Management** (apps that can update or delete other apps), **Local Network**, **Bluetooth** and **Location Services**. Use the same rule: keep what you know and use, and ask IT about names you don't recognise.
- **Share less with Apple and Siri.** In **Privacy & Security > Analytics & Improvements**, you can turn off **Share Mac Analytics** and **Improve Siri & Dictation**. Your Mac and Siri keep working the same. It just sends less about how you use them.
- **Make it a habit.** Repeat this check a few times a year, and right after installing new software. Most apps ask for one of these permissions on first use, and it's easy to click **Allow** without thinking.

#### 12. Review apps that start by themselves

**Why this matters:** Some apps (and some nasty software) set themselves to run every time you log in, quietly, in the background. A quick look shows you what's running without you knowing.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > General > Login Items & Extensions**. (On macOS 14 Sonoma and earlier it's called **Login Items**.)
2. Under **Open at Login**, look at the apps that open when you log in. To remove one you don't want, select it and click the **minus (–)** button.
3. Under **App Background Activity** (called **Allow in the Background** on Sequoia), look at the apps allowed to run in the background.
4. Turn off any you know you no longer use. If you don't recognise a name, take a screenshot and ask IT before turning it off.
5. Further down you'll see **Extensions**. These are add-ons that plug extra features into your Mac (for example, into Finder or the Share menu). You can click through them, but leave them as they are unless you're sure. Ask IT if something looks odd.

**How to check it worked:** Restart your Mac. Only apps you expect open on their own.

**Heads-up:**
- You need to be an administrator to change these settings. If you've switched to a standard account (step 21), your Mac will ask for the admin name and password.
- Turning off a background item for, say, a cloud-storage app means it won't sync until you open it. If something stops syncing, turn its switch back on.

**Going further:**
- **See everything that starts by itself, not just what Settings shows.** **KnockKnock**, a free tool from **objective-see.org**, lists every item set to start automatically, including some that **Login Items & Extensions** doesn't show. The trade-off: the list is long and mostly normal Apple items. Don't delete anything from it. If a name looks odd, take a screenshot and ask IT. Check the site says it supports your version of macOS. On a work Mac, ask IT first.
- **Get a warning the moment something sets itself to start.** **BlockBlock**, also free from **objective-see.org**, pops up when an app tries to add itself to your start-up list, and lets you block it. The trade-off: it also pops up during normal installs and updates, so you need to read each alert, and it needs **Full Disk Access** to work. On a work Mac, ask IT first.
- **Don't swipe away "Background Items Added".** Your Mac shows this notification when an app adds itself to run in the background. If you just installed that app, it's normal. If you didn't install anything, open **System Settings > General > Login Items & Extensions** and check the list.
- **Peek in the folders where start-up files live.** In Finder, choose **Go > Go to Folder** and type **/Library/LaunchAgents**, then **/Library/LaunchDaemons**, then **~/Library/LaunchAgents**. Names like **com.google...** or **com.microsoft...**, from companies whose apps you use, are normal. Don't move or delete anything here yourself: ask IT about names you don't recognise.

#### 13. Review browser extensions

**Why this matters:** Browser extensions can see every website you visit and sometimes what you type on them. Bad or abandoned extensions are a common way passwords get stolen.

**For each browser you use**, open its extensions list, then turn off or remove anything you don't recognise or don't use.

**Safari**
1. Open Safari. In the menu bar click **Safari > Settings**, then the **Extensions** tab.
2. To turn an extension off, untick the box next to its name. This is safe and easy, and you can tick it again later.
3. Removing it completely usually means deleting the app it came with. If you click **Uninstall**, Safari will probably tell you the extension is part of an app and offer **Show in Finder**. Only move that app to the Trash if you're sure you don't need it. If you're unsure, leave it unticked and ask IT.

**Google Chrome**
1. Click the **three dots** in the top-right, then **Extensions > Manage Extensions**.
2. Click **Remove** on anything you don't need.

**Microsoft Edge**
1. Click the **three dots** in the top-right, then **Extensions > Manage extensions**.
2. Click **Remove** on anything you don't need.

**Firefox**
1. Click the **three lines** in the top-right, then **Add-ons and themes**, then **Extensions**.
2. Click the **three dots** next to an extension and choose **Remove**.

**How to check it worked:** Each browser only lists (or only has switched on) extensions you know and use, such as your password manager.

**Heads-up:** Only install extensions from the browser's official store, and only ones you really need.

**Going further:**
- **Let extensions see only the sites they need.** In Safari, open **Safari > Settings > Extensions**, select an extension, click **Edit Websites**, and set **When visiting other websites** to **Ask**. In Chrome, click **Details** on the extension in **Manage Extensions**, then set **Site access** to **On click** or **On specific sites**. The trade-off: you'll click to allow the extension more often.
- **Use a separate browser profile for banking.** A profile is like a second, clean copy of your browser with its own cookies and extensions. In Safari, go to **Safari > Settings > Profiles** and click **New Profile**, then turn on only your password manager in that profile's **Extensions** tab. In Chrome, click your profile picture in the top-right and choose the option to add a new profile. The trade-off: you have to switch to that window for banking.
- **Fewer is safer.** Extensions are sometimes sold to new owners who quietly add tracking or worse. If an extension suddenly asks for new permissions after an update, or you can't remember why you have it, remove it. Most people only need their password manager.
- **On a work Mac, ask IT before adding any new extension.** Some companies only allow approved ones.

#### 14. Turn on Find My Mac

**Why this matters:** If your Mac is lost or stolen, you can see where it is, make it play a sound, lock it or erase it from another device. It also stops a thief from simply wiping and reusing it.

**Check with IT first if this is a company-owned Mac.** Turning on Find My locks the Mac to whichever Apple Account you use (this is called **Activation Lock**). If that's your personal account, IT may not be able to erase, reset or set up the Mac later until you turn Find My off. Ask IT which Apple Account to use, or whether they want you to wait.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings** and click **your name** at the top of the sidebar.
2. Click **iCloud**.
3. Click **See All** (next to "Saved to iCloud"), then click **Find My Mac**.
4. Click **Turn On**. If asked to let Find My Mac use your Mac's location, click **Allow**.
5. Make sure location is on: open **System Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services**, and turn on **Location Services**.

**How to check it worked:** Go to icloud.com/find on another device, or open the **Find My** app on your iPhone. Your Mac should appear on the map.

**If you don't see this:** If you're not signed in with an Apple Account, check with IT which account to use, then click **Sign in** at the top of the sidebar. If the button says **Show More Apps** instead of **See All**, click that. If Find My Mac says it's on but your Mac doesn't appear, turn it off and on again, then restart.

**Heads-up:** Before you sell or hand back this Mac, or if IT asks to set it up again, you'll need to turn Find My off (with your Apple Account password). Otherwise nobody else can use or reset it.

**Going further:**
- **Learn the buttons before you need them.** Open the **Find My** app on your iPhone, tap **Devices**, then your Mac. Look (don't tap) at **Mark As Lost**, which locks the Mac and shows a message with your phone number, and **Erase This Device**. When you're upset after a theft, it helps to already know where they are.
- **If it's stolen, don't remove it from your account.** Keep the Mac listed in Find My, even after you erase it. That keeps **Activation Lock** on, so a thief can't set it up as their own. Only remove it once you've sold it, given it away or handed it back, as described in this step.
- **Save your serial number somewhere else.** Click the **Apple menu > About This Mac** and copy the **Serial number** into your password manager, with a photo of the receipt. Police, insurers and IT will ask for it.
- **Check Activation Lock is really on.** Hold **Option**, click the **Apple menu**, choose **System Information**, then click **Hardware**. **Activation Lock Status** should say **Enabled**.

#### 15. Stop Safari opening downloads automatically

**Why this matters:** By default Safari opens some downloaded files (like PDFs and zip files) by itself. Turning this off means nothing opens until you choose to open it.

**Steps**
1. Open Safari. In the menu bar click **Safari > Settings**, then the **General** tab.
2. Untick **Open “safe” files after downloading**.
3. Click the **Security** tab and make sure **Warn when visiting a fraudulent website** is ticked. Safari then warns you before it opens a known scam site.

**How to check it worked:** Download a PDF. It should appear in your Downloads folder without opening. In **Safari > Settings > Security**, **Warn when visiting a fraudulent website** is ticked.

**Heads-up:** You'll need to double-click downloaded files yourself. That's it.

**Going further:**
- **Make websites ask before they download anything.** Open **Safari > Settings > Websites**, click **Downloads** in the list, and set **When visiting other websites** to **Ask**. A page then can't drop a file on your Mac without you clicking. The trade-off: one extra click each time on sites you haven't allowed.
- **Look before you open.** Select a downloaded file in Finder and press the **Space bar**. This shows a preview (Apple calls it Quick Look) without opening it in an app. If a "PDF" won't preview, or turns out to be an app, delete it.
- **Check where a file came from.** Select the file in Finder, choose **File > Get Info**, and look at **Where from**. If an "invoice" came from a web address you don't recognise, don't open it. Not every file shows this line.
- **Never paste a command a website gives you.** A common Mac trick is a fake "I'm not a robot" or install page that tells you to open **Terminal** and paste something. The **Staying safe online guide, step 7** shows what it looks like.

#### 16. Remove unknown profiles

**Why this matters:** A "profile" is a small settings file that can change how your Mac works, for example routing your internet traffic somewhere else. Scammers sometimes trick people into installing them. On an unmanaged Mac there usually shouldn't be any, unless IT sent you one.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > General > Device Management**.
2. Look at the list. If it says there are no profiles, you're done.
3. If you see a profile, click it to see who made it.
4. If it's from IT, or from an app you trust (like a VPN you use), leave it.
5. If you don't recognise it, **take a screenshot and contact IT before removing it.** If IT says it's safe to remove, select it and click the **minus (–)** button, then confirm.

**How to check it worked:** Only profiles you and IT recognise are listed.

**If you don't see this:** On older versions (macOS 14 Sonoma and earlier) this was in **System Settings > Privacy & Security > Profiles**. If there are no profiles at all, **Device Management** may not appear in the **General** list. That means you have none, which is fine.

**Heads-up:** Removing a profile removes everything it set up, such as a Wi-Fi network or email account.

**Going further:**
- **Check the other places your internet can be redirected.** Open **System Settings > Network** and look at **VPN & Filters** (on some versions just **VPN**). Anything listed should be IT's filter (step 20), a VPN you chose, or a security app you installed on purpose. Then click **Wi-Fi**, then **Details** next to your network, then **Proxies**: everything should be off unless IT set it. Take a screenshot and ask IT about anything else.
- **Check your iPhone too.** Scammers who get one device often try the other. The **iPhone guide, step 13a** shows where profiles live there.
- **Look again after a scare.** If someone "helped" you over the phone, or you installed something from an email or pop-up, repeat this step and step 12 the same day.

#### 17. Show file extensions in Finder

**Why this matters:** A file named "Invoice.pdf" could secretly be "Invoice.pdf.app", which is a program, not a document. Showing the full name helps you spot fakes.

**Steps**
1. Click on an empty part of your desktop, or open a **Finder** window.
2. In the menu bar click **Finder > Settings**.
3. Click the **Advanced** tab.
4. Tick **Show all filename extensions**.

**How to check it worked:** Files now show endings like **.pdf**, **.docx** or **.app**.

**Heads-up:** Be careful not to change the ending when renaming a file. If you do, your Mac will ask whether you're sure. Click the button that says **Keep** followed by the original ending (for example, **Keep .pdf**) to leave it as it was.

**Going further:**
- **Show what kind of file it really is.** In a Finder window, choose **View > as List**. If there's no **Kind** column, Control-click a column heading and tick **Kind**. A real document says something like **PDF document**. If it says **Application** and you expected a document, don't open it.
- **See where a file really is.** Choose **View > Show Path Bar**. The bar at the bottom shows which folder or disk a file is in. If a "document" turns out to be on a disk you just downloaded, rather than in your Downloads folder, be careful before opening it.
- **Tidy up installers.** After installing an app, eject the installer (the **.dmg** disk in the Finder sidebar) and move the **.dmg** file to the Trash. Old installers left in Downloads are easy to open by mistake later.

#### 18. Check your Mac's built-in protection is on

**Why this matters:** Your Mac has a built-in safety lock called **System Integrity Protection** (SIP for short). It stops anything, even malware with your password, from changing the core parts of macOS. It's on by default and should stay on. This step is just a quick check.

**Steps (the easy way)**
1. Hold down the **Option** key, click the **Apple menu**, and choose **System Information**.
2. In the left list, under **Software**, click **Software** itself.
3. Look for **System Integrity Protection**. It should say **Enabled**.

**For the curious (optional)**
If you're comfortable with it, you can also open the **Terminal** app (in Applications > Utilities), type `csrutil status` and press Return. It should say **System Integrity Protection status: enabled.** Then close Terminal. Don't type anything else there.

**What about Startup Security?** Your Mac also has a setting called Startup Security, which controls what your Mac is allowed to start up from (for example, blocking an unknown USB drive from taking over at startup). It's on by default. IT checks this; you don't need to do anything.

**How to check it worked:** It says **Enabled**.

**If it says Disabled:** Don't try to fix it yourself. It has been switched off at some point. Please tell IT so they can turn it back on.

**Going further:**
- **Look at Startup Security without restarting (Apple silicon).** Hold **Option**, click the **Apple menu**, choose **System Information**, then under **Hardware** click **Controller**. Look for **Secure Boot**: it should say **Full Security**. If it says anything else, tell IT. Don't try to change it yourself.
- **Know what's not there.** Newer Macs with Apple silicon don't have the old "firmware password". The closest feature, **Recovery Lock**, can only be set by a company's management system. Your login password, FileVault (step 2) and Find My (step 14) are what protect this Mac. On an older Intel Mac, ask IT whether they want a firmware password.
- **Make every accessory ask first (Mac laptops with Apple silicon).** In **System Settings > Privacy & Security**, find **Allow accessories to connect** and choose **Ask Every Time** (on some versions **Always Ask**). Never choose **Always Allow**. A cable or gadget plugged in while you're away then can't connect until you approve it, even one you've used before. The trade-off: you approve your dock, hub or backup drive each time. Desktop Macs don't have this setting.
- **For people at higher risk, there's Lockdown Mode.** It's step 23, and only for when IT tells you to.

#### 19. Turn on encrypted Time Machine backups

**Why this matters:** If your Mac breaks or is stolen, a backup means you don't lose your files. Encrypting the backup means nobody can read it if the drive goes missing.

**No external drive yet?** Do the rest of the guide, and come back to this step as soon as you have one. If you already have a drive, do this step first, before the others.

**What you need:** An external drive, ideally at least twice the size of your Mac's storage (for example, 2TB for a 1TB Mac). **Setting up a new drive for Time Machine may erase everything on it**, so use an empty one.

**Steps (setting up a new drive)**
1. Plug the drive into your Mac.
2. Open **System Settings > General > Time Machine**.
3. Click **Add Backup Disk** (or the **+** button).
4. Choose your drive and click **Set Up Disk**.
5. Turn on **Encrypt Backup**.
6. Create a password for the backup. **Save this password in your password manager.** Without it you can't restore your files.
7. Click **Done**. If asked to erase the disk, only click **Erase** if there's nothing on it you need.

**Already have an unencrypted Time Machine drive?** You don't need to erase it.
1. Plug it in and open a **Finder** window.
2. Control-click (or right-click) the backup drive in the Finder sidebar, then choose **Encrypt**.
3. Type a password in **Encryption password** and **Verify password**, add a hint, and click **Encrypt Disk**.
4. Save the password in your password manager. Your existing backups are kept.

**How to check it worked:** **Time Machine** shows your drive with the date of the latest backup. Plug the drive in regularly (once a week is a good habit).

**Heads-up:** Encrypting an existing drive can take hours. Keep it plugged in. (This only applies to a drive plugged into your Mac. A network backup drive, such as a Time Capsule, does have to start over when you encrypt it.)

**Going further:**
- **Keep a second copy somewhere else.** A fire or burglary can take the Mac and the backup drive together. Time Machine can use two drives and takes turns between them: add the second one in **System Settings > General > Time Machine** with **Add Backup Disk**, turn on **Encrypt Backup** for it too, and keep it at another place (work or a relative's house). Swap them every few weeks. The trade-off: a second drive costs money and you have to remember to swap.
- **Test that you can get a file back.** Open a folder in Finder, then open the **Time Machine** app (in **Applications**). Go back in time, pick a file and click **Restore**. A backup you've never restored from is only a hope.
- **Unplug the drive after the backup finishes.** Harmful software that scrambles your files can also reach a drive that's always plugged in. Eject it in Finder, then unplug it. Plug it back in at least once a week, as in the main step.
- **iCloud Drive isn't a backup.** If you delete or damage a file, iCloud copies that change to all your devices. A paid online backup service is a good offsite copy, but on a work Mac ask IT first: company files may not be allowed there.

#### 20. Install the web filtering profile from IT (if IT sent you one)

**Why this matters:** IT may give you a small "web filtering" profile. It blocks known scam and malware websites before they even load, whatever browser you use. (Technically it changes your Mac's DNS, which is like the internet's phone book.)

**If IT has sent you a filtering profile, install it now. If not, skip this step.**

**Only do this with a link or file that IT sent you directly.** Never install a profile from a website pop-up or an unexpected email.

**Steps**
1. IT will send you a link or a file. It usually ends in **.mobileconfig**.
2. Open the link, or double-click the file. You may see a message that a profile was downloaded and needs to be reviewed in System Settings.
3. Open **System Settings > General > Device Management**.
4. Under **Downloaded**, double-click the profile.
5. Read the summary and check it matches what IT told you (the name, and who it's from).
6. Click **Install** (or **Continue**, then **Install**). Enter your Mac password if asked.

**How to check it worked:** The profile now appears in **Device Management** as installed. IT may give you a test website that should show as blocked.

**If you don't see this:** If the profile isn't listed under **Downloaded**, open the link or file from IT again.

**Heads-up:** Occasionally a safe site might be blocked by mistake. Tell IT and they can unblock it.

**Going further:**
- **Keep Safari's own scam warning on.** You turned it on in step 15. It works with IT's filter or without one, so leave it on.
- **Check the filter now and then.** Open **System Settings > General > Device Management** and make sure IT's profile is still listed. If IT gave you a test website, open it every few months: it should still be blocked. If it isn't, tell IT.
- **Ask IT before turning on iCloud Private Relay.** Private Relay (part of the paid iCloud+ plan) hides your Safari browsing from the network you're on. It can also change how web filtering works. Ask IT whether they're happy for you to use both.
- **If a site is blocked, don't work around it.** Don't remove the profile, switch to another DNS service or use a VPN to reach a blocked site. Tell IT which site it was, and they can check it and unblock it if it's safe.

### Nice-to-have

#### 21. Use a standard (non-admin) account day to day

**Why this matters:** An "admin" account can install software and change any setting. If you're tricked into running something bad while using an admin account, it can do much more damage. Using a "standard" account for everyday work, and a separate admin account only when needed, adds a safety step.

**Is this for me?** It's a great habit, but it is the most fiddly step in this guide. If it feels like too much, skip it. Everything else still counts. If you do it, set aside 20 minutes and have your passwords to hand.

**Steps**
1. **Create a separate admin account:**
   1. Open **System Settings > Users & Groups**.
   2. Click **Add User** and enter your password if asked.
   3. Click the pop-up menu next to **New User** and choose **Administrator**.
   4. Name it something clear like "Admin". Give it a strong password that's different from your daily one. Save that password in your password manager.
   5. Click **Create User**.
   6. **Don't sign this Admin account in to an Apple Account or iCloud.** It's only for typing in when your Mac asks for an administrator.
2. **Test the Admin account:** Restart your Mac. At the login screen, choose the Admin account and log in. This checks the password works and that the account can unlock the Mac at startup. If the Admin account doesn't appear on the startup screen, or its password doesn't work there, stop here and contact IT.
3. **While logged in to the Admin account, make your usual account standard:**
   1. Open **System Settings > Users & Groups**.
   2. Click the **i** button next to *your usual* account.
   3. Turn off **Allow this user to administer this computer**.
   4. Click **OK**, then restart your Mac and log back in to your usual account.

**How to check it worked:** In **Users & Groups**, your usual account now shows **Standard** and the new one shows **Admin**.

**Heads-up:**
- When you install apps or change some settings, your Mac will ask for an **administrator** name and password. Type the Admin account's name and password, not your own.
- Don't make your usual account standard (part 3 above) until you've confirmed the Admin account works. If you get this wrong you could lock yourself out of admin tasks.
- Some apps that install background tools may need the admin password more often.
- If you get stuck, stop and contact IT.

**Going further:**
- **Never use the Admin account for daily life.** Only type its name and password into the boxes that ask for an administrator. Don't log in to it to browse, read email or open downloads. If you do, you lose most of the benefit of this step.
- **Keep a second copy of the Admin password away from the Mac.** If your password manager only works on this Mac and something goes wrong, you can't get in to read it. Keep a paper copy locked away, for example with your FileVault recovery key (step 2).
- **Give everyone their own standard account.** If family members use this Mac, give each of them their own **Standard** account, not yours and not the Admin one. Then their downloads and mistakes mostly stay in their own account. Check **Users & Groups** now and then: only the Admin account should show **Admin** (step 6 covers unknown accounts).

#### 22. Set a lock screen message

**Why this matters:** If you lose your Mac, an honest finder can see how to return it.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Lock Screen**.
2. Turn on **Show message when locked**, then click **Set**.
3. Type something like "If found, please call [work phone number]." Don't include your home address.
4. Click **Done**.

**How to check it worked:** Lock your screen (Control + Command + Q). Your message appears.

**Going further:**
- **Hide account names at the login screen.** Open **System Settings > Lock Screen** and set **Login window shows** to **Name and password**. Someone who picks up your Mac then has to guess your account name as well as your password. The trade-off: you type your name too. On a Mac with FileVault, the first screen after start-up still shows the names of people who can unlock it.
- **Add a label for when the battery is flat.** The lock screen message only shows when the Mac is switched on. A small sticker or engraving on the bottom with a work phone number or email works even with a dead battery. Don't put your home address on it.
- **You can also send a message after it's lost.** **Mark As Lost** in the **Find My** app (step 14) shows a message and phone number on the screen, even if you never set one here.

### Only if IT tells you to

These are strong protections for people who are at higher risk, such as those who handle very sensitive information or may be targeted personally. They have real downsides, so **only turn them on if IT asks you to.**

**One exception:** security keys (step 25) on a personal Apple Account. If you're comfortable with tech, you can add them yourself after reading the downsides. If you use that Apple Account on any device you use for work, ask IT first.

#### 23. Lockdown Mode

**What it is:** An extreme protection mode for people who might be targeted by sophisticated spyware. It switches off many features that attackers could use.

**Before you start:** If IT sent you a web filtering profile (step 20), install it **first**. You can't install profiles while Lockdown Mode is on. If you need one later, turn Lockdown Mode off, install it, then turn Lockdown Mode back on.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings > Privacy & Security**.
2. Scroll down to **Lockdown Mode** and click **Turn On**.
3. Enter your password if asked, then click **Turn On & Restart**.

**Downsides:** Some websites load slower or look broken. Most message attachments are blocked. FaceTime calls are blocked from people you haven't called in the past 30 days. Shared albums and some device connections stop working. You need to turn it on separately on each of your devices (Mac, iPhone, iPad). Phone calls, normal messages and emergency features keep working.

**How to check it worked:** **Privacy & Security** shows Lockdown Mode as on.

#### 24. Advanced Data Protection for iCloud

**What it is:** Normally, Apple can help you recover some iCloud data (like Photos and Notes) if you lose access. With Advanced Data Protection, almost all your iCloud data is locked so that only your devices can open it, not even Apple.

**Before you start:** Update **all** your Apple devices first. You must set up a **recovery contact** (a trusted person) or a **recovery key**, or both.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings**, click **your name**, then **iCloud**.
2. Click **Advanced Data Protection**, then **Turn On**.
3. Follow the steps on screen to set up your recovery method.

**Downsides:** **If you lose access to your devices and your recovery method, Apple cannot get your data back.** Viewing iCloud data on the iCloud.com website needs extra steps. Some features may not be available in every country.

**How to check it worked:** **iCloud > Advanced Data Protection** shows it as on.

#### 25. Security keys for your Apple Account

**What it is:** A security key is a small physical device (like a USB stick) that you plug in or tap to sign in to your Apple Account. It replaces the six-digit codes and stops fake "Apple" websites from stealing your sign-in.

**What you need:** At least two security keys that work with Apple devices (Apple calls them "FIDO Certified"). For a work account, IT will tell you which to use.

**Steps**
1. Open **System Settings**, click **your name**, then **Sign-In & Security**.
2. Click **Two-Factor Authentication**.
3. Next to **Security Keys**, click **Set Up** and follow the steps to add both keys.
4. At the end you'll see a list of devices signed in to your Apple Account. Keep the ones you use; sign out any you don't recognise.

**Downsides:**
- **If you lose all your keys, you could be locked out of your Apple Account.** Keep the spare somewhere safe, away from the main one.
- Every device signed in to your Apple Account must be on macOS 13.2, iOS 16.3 or iPadOS 16.3 or later.
- Devices you haven't used for more than 90 days get signed out during setup. If one can't be updated, you won't be able to sign back in on it.
- You'll need a key to sign in on new devices or on the web.

**How to check it worked:** **Sign-In & Security > Two-Factor Authentication** lists your security keys.

Thanks for taking the time. Your Mac is now much harder to break into and much easier to recover if something goes wrong.

- Install updates when your Mac asks.
- Lock your screen when you walk away (Control + Command + Q).
- Never install anything a pop-up or unexpected email tells you to.
- If something looks wrong, ask IT (see "How to reach IT" at the top). There are no silly questions.

## Staying Safe Online: A Step-by-Step Guide

### What this is

Most online crime doesn't start with a clever hacker breaking in. It starts with a message, a call or a pop-up that tricks a normal person into doing one small thing: tapping a link, reading out a code, or sending money. This guide shows you what those tricks look like, and the habits that stop them.

It's for everyone: you, your family, your parents, your kids. You don't need to be "good with tech." Words in **bold** are either words you'll see on screen, or the most important points to remember. A path like **Settings > Apps > Phone** means: open the Settings app, tap **Apps**, then tap **Phone**.

## Part 1: Don'ts

### 1. Don't tap links or open attachments you weren't expecting

**Why this matters:** A link (blue or underlined words you can tap) in a fake message usually leads to a copy of a real login page. Whatever you type there goes straight to the criminal. This trick is called **phishing**: a fake message or web page that fishes for your password or money. An attachment (a file that comes with an email or message) can install harmful software on your computer.

**What it looks like:**
- A text saying a **package couldn't be delivered**, or that you owe an **unpaid toll**, a **parking fine** or a **DMV fee**. It tells you to pay "today" to avoid a penalty.
- An email that looks like it's from your bank, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, PayPal or the IRS: "Your account is locked," "Unusual sign-in," "Your refund is ready."
- A "shared document," "voicemail" or "invoice" you weren't expecting, even from someone you know. Their account may have been hacked.
- A "renewal" invoice for antivirus or computer support that you never bought, with a phone number to "cancel."
- The same tricks in WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram or LinkedIn messages.

**Do this instead:** Don't tap. Open the company's own app, or type their web address yourself (see step 18), and check your account there. If something really needs your attention, you'll see it there too. If the message came from someone you know, ask them by phone or in person whether they sent it.

**Heads-up:** The IRS doesn't start contact by text, email or social media about a bill or refund. Your bank won't text you a link to "unlock" your account. The US Postal Service only texts you if you asked for tracking updates yourself, and even then its texts never contain a link. A text "from USPS" with a link is a scam.

**If it already happened:**
- **You only tapped the link and closed it:** if your device is up to date (step 22), you're very likely fine. Don't type anything into that page, don't download anything it offers, and expect more scam texts. Delete and report the message (step 26).
- **You typed a password:** change that password straight away, on the real site or app. If you use it anywhere else, change it there too. Turn on two-factor (step 16).
- **You typed card or bank details:** call your bank on the number on the back of your card.
- **You opened an attachment on a work computer, or something odd happened after you opened it:** contact IT (or your trusted helper).

**Going further:**
- **Give each shop its own email address.** Apple's **Hide My Email** (part of the paid iCloud+ plan) or **SimpleLogin** (by Proton; the free plan gives you 10 addresses) makes a different forwarding address for each site you sign up to. If a "bank" email arrives at the address you only gave a shoe shop, you know straight away it's fake, and you can switch that address off. The trade-off: you have more addresses to keep track of, and you rely on that service to pass your mail on. Your password manager can remember which address goes with which site.
- **Check the email's "proof of sender".** In **Gmail on a computer**, click the three dots next to the reply arrow, then **Show original**. Near the top you'll see **SPF**, **DKIM** and **DMARC**. **DMARC: PASS** means the email really came from the web address after the @ sign. **FAIL** next to a big company's name is a strong scam sign. A pass only proves where it came from, not that the sender is honest: a lookalike address like **paypa1.com** can pass too.
- **Let a scanning site open a link for you.** If you really need to know where a link goes, copy it (don't open it) and paste it into **urlscan.io**, which visits the page for you and shows a screenshot. It may ask you to make a free account. Scans are public by default, so never scan a link that contains your name, email address or a reset code. **VirusTotal** can check a suspicious file, but files you upload can be shared with security companies, so never upload anything private. When in doubt, just delete the message.
- **Block known scam sites for the whole house.** A filtering DNS service quietly refuses to open known scam and malware sites on every device that uses it. **Quad9** is free. **NextDNS** is free up to 300,000 look-ups a month, then stops filtering until the next month (Pro is about $2 a month). You set it up once, on your home Wi-Fi router or on each device. The trade-off: once in a while it blocks a real site, and someone has to know how to unblock it. On a work computer or phone, don't change this: IT may already filter for you (see the **Mac guide, step 20** and **iPhone guide, step 13b**), and changing it can break work tools.

### 2. Don't share a verification code with anyone

**Why this matters:** A verification code (also called a one-time code or a login code) is the last lock on your account. If a criminal already has your password, the code is the only thing they're missing. If you read it out, they're in.

**What it looks like:**
- Someone calls "from your bank's fraud team." To "stop a fraudulent payment," they're sending you a code, and you just need to read it back.
- A message from "Apple Support" or "Microsoft" asking you to confirm a code to "secure your account."
- Someone selling something online asks for "the code Google just sent you" to "prove you're real." If you give them the code, they can use your phone number to set up an account in your name, or get into yours.
- A friend or relative messages you: "I sent you a code by mistake, can you send it to me?" Their account was probably hacked.
- Someone gives **you** a code and asks you to type it into a real Microsoft or Google page (for example **microsoft.com/devicelogin**) to "connect" a meeting, a document or a support session. That signs them in as you.

**Do this instead:** Never read, type or forward a code to anyone. **If someone contacted you (a call, text, email or message) and asks for a code, it's a scam.** Not your bank, not Apple, not Microsoft, not the police, and not IT will ask. A code is only ever for you to type into a sign-in screen **that you opened yourself**. Don't type a code someone else gave you into a sign-in page either. Read the code message itself: it often says "Don't share this code."

**Heads-up:** There are two exceptions. Both only apply when **you** started the contact.
- If you're someone's Apple **Recovery Contact** (see the **iPhone guide, step 6d**), your iPhone can show a recovery code for them. Only give it to them in person, or on a call **you** started to the number you already have for them.
- When **you** call your bank or phone company, on the number from your card, statement or bill, they may text you a code and ask you to read it back to check it's you. That's OK on that call only. If they called you, or a text asked you to call a number, it's never OK.

**If it already happened:**
- Hang up or stop replying.
- Sign in to that account yourself (in the official app, or by typing the address) and change the password.
- Check the account's list of signed-in devices and sign out anything you don't recognise. This is usually under **Security** or **Devices** in the account's settings. If you can't find it, that's fine. Changing the password is the most important part. Ask IT or a trusted helper to check the rest with you.
- If it was your bank, call the number on the back of your card. If it was your work account, contact IT straight away.

**Going further:**
- **Need codes less often.** Every account you move to a **passkey** or a **security key** (steps 15 and 16) is an account with no code to trick out of you. A passkey only works on the real website, so even a convincing caller can't use it. Start with email, Apple, Google, Microsoft and your bank. The trade-off: you need a plan for a lost phone or key, so keep backup codes somewhere safe or register a second key.
- **Read the permission screen, not just the code box.** A close cousin of this trick is a page that says **"[Some app] wants to access your Google Account"** (or Microsoft account) with an **Allow** button. Tapping **Allow** gives that app your email or files with no code needed, and changing your password may not take it back. If you didn't go looking for that app, tap **Cancel**.
- **Clear out old connections a few times a year.** Step 17 shows where to find the list of apps with access to your email. Do the same for your Google and Microsoft accounts even if you don't use their email. For Google, it's at **myaccount.google.com**, then **Security**, then **Your connections to third-party apps & services**. Remove anything you don't recognise or no longer use.
- **At work, tell IT if anyone gave you a code to type in.** Pages like **microsoft.com/devicelogin** are real Microsoft pages, which is why the trick works. Many IT teams can switch that sign-in method off for your office, but only if they know it's being used against you.

### 3. Don't approve a sign-in or password reset you didn't start

**Why this matters:** If a criminal has your password, they may try to sign in and hope you tap **Allow** or **Approve** on the pop-up that appears on your phone. Some send dozens of pop-ups, hoping you'll tap one just to make them stop.

**What it looks like:**
- Your iPhone keeps showing **Reset Password** or sign-in pop-ups for your Apple Account, over and over, and you didn't ask for them.
- Your phone buzzes with "Are you trying to sign in?" from Microsoft, Google or your bank app while you're not signing in to anything.
- Soon after, someone calls pretending to be from Apple or your bank and says, "We saw suspicious activity. Just approve the next prompt and read me the code."

**Do this instead:** If you didn't start it, tap **Don't Allow**, **Deny** or **No, it's not me**. Every time. If it was a **sign-in** request, someone probably has your password, so change that account's password from a device you trust. If it was only **Reset Password** pop-ups, they may just know your email or phone number. Denying them is the main thing, though changing the password does no harm. If the pop-ups keep coming, or someone calls about them, it's a sign someone is targeting you. Hang up and tell IT.

**Heads-up:** Apple won't call you about these pop-ups. If your iPhone shows a sign-in alert, it also shows a rough location on a map. That location is often wrong, so don't use it to decide. Use this rule: if you didn't start it, deny it. If you change your Apple Account password on an iPhone, it may make you wait an hour first (**iPhone guide, step 8**). That's normal.

**If it already happened:** Change that account's password straight away, from a device you trust. Check the list of signed-in devices and remove any you don't recognise (for your Apple Account, see the **iPhone guide, step 6c**). For other accounts, this is usually under **Security** or **Devices** in the account's settings. If you can't find it, changing the password is the most important part. Ask IT or a trusted helper to check the rest with you. If it was a work account, contact IT now.

**Going further:**
- **Switch to sign-ins that can't be approved by accident.** With a **passkey** or a **security key** (a small key such as a YubiKey that you plug in or tap), signing in needs your device or the key right there with the real website. Nobody can flood you with "Approve?" pop-ups for it. The trade-off: keys cost money (roughly $25 to $60 each), you should buy two in case one is lost, and you have to keep them somewhere safe. For your Apple Account, read the **iPhone guide, step 31** first, including its downsides. If you use that Apple Account on any device you use for work, ask IT first.
- **Use the number check if your app has one.** Microsoft Authenticator and some other apps show a number on the sign-in screen that you have to type into your phone. If your phone asks for a number and you're not looking at a sign-in screen showing one, someone else is trying to sign in. Deny it.
- **Look at the account's own security history.** Google: **myaccount.google.com/security-checkup** shows your devices and recent security events. Microsoft: **account.microsoft.com**, then **Security**, then look for **sign-in activity**. If you see sign-ins or attempts you don't recognise, someone probably has your password. Change it and move that account to a passkey. Locations shown there are often rough, so judge by "was this me?", not by the town.
- **For people who are being targeted.** If you're a public figure, handle large sums of money, or keep getting these pop-ups, Google's free **Advanced Protection Program** makes your Google account accept only passkeys or security keys. The trade-off: it's strict. Getting back in after losing your keys can take several days, and some third-party apps can no longer reach your Gmail or Drive. On a work Google account, ask IT first.

### 4. Don't trust caller ID, urgent calls or voicemails

**Why this matters:** Caller ID is easy to fake. A call can show your bank's real name and number, the police, or even a family member, and still be a criminal. Voices can be faked too: a short clip of someone talking, from a video or voicemail, can be enough for AI to copy their voice convincingly. That sounds alarming, but one simple habit beats it every time: hang up and call back on a number you already know.

**What it looks like:**
- "This is your bank's fraud department. Your account has been compromised. Move your money to this safe account."
- "This is the sheriff's office. You missed jury duty. Pay the fine now, or we'll arrest you."
- "Social Security" or "Medicare" calling to say your number has been suspended, or that you need a new card and must confirm your details. Social Security numbers aren't suspended, and these offices don't call to ask for payment.
- A crying grandchild, child or partner: "I've been in an accident, I'm in jail, please send money, and don't tell Mom."
- A text from an unknown number: "Hi Mom, I dropped my phone, this is my new number." Then a request for money. Call them on the number you already have.
- Your boss, sounding exactly like your boss, asking you to pay something urgently or buy gift cards.
- A voicemail asking you to "call back immediately" on a number they give you.

**Do this instead:** Hang up. Then **call back on a number you look up yourself**: the number on the back of your card, on your bill, in the company's official app, or in your own contacts. For family or your boss, call them on the number you already have, or ask your code word (step 21). A real bank, government office or family member will understand.

**Heads-up:** On iPhone with iOS 26 or later, go to **Settings > Apps > Phone**. Under **Screen Unknown Callers**, choose **Ask Reason for Calling**. Your iPhone asks callers who aren't in your contacts to say who they are and why they're calling before it rings. On older versions, you can turn on **Silence Unknown Callers** in the same place. It cuts down scam calls, but caller ID can still be faked, so the rule above still applies. Trade-off: real callers who aren't in your contacts, like a hospital, pharmacy or delivery driver, may hang up or go to voicemail. Save the numbers that matter in your contacts first, and check your voicemail. **If you don't see this:** open **Settings** and type "Unknown Callers" in the search box at the top.

**If it already happened:** If you gave money, card numbers or account details, call your bank right away on the number on your card. See "If you think you've been scammed" near the end of this guide.

**Going further:**
- **Write your call-back list before you need it.** Save your bank's fraud number (from the back of your card), your card companies, your doctor, your phone company and your close family in your contacts, with clear names like **"Bank fraud line (from card)"**. When a scary call comes, you call from that list, not from memory or a search.
- **Turn on your phone company's free scam-call filter.** T-Mobile has **Scam Shield**, Verizon has **Call Filter**, and AT&T has **ActiveArmor**, each with a free basic version in the carrier's app. They label or block many known scam numbers before your phone rings. Paid upgrades exist, but the free version is enough for most people. They miss some calls, so the hang-up-and-call-back rule still applies.
- **Put a password on your voicemail.** If your voicemail has no PIN, or still has the default one, criminals can sometimes get into it and grab messages that read out your sign-in codes. Open **Settings** and type "Voicemail Password" in the search box, or use your carrier's app. Don't use your birthday or your phone passcode.
- **Ask your bank for a phone password.** Some banks let you add a spoken password, or a note on your account, that must be given before any change is made by phone. This stops a criminal who calls them pretending to be you. Store it in your password manager. Your phone company's account PIN is covered in the **iPhone guide, step 11b**.
- **Keep your voicemail greeting short.** A generic greeting ("You've reached this number, please leave a message") gives voice-copying tools less of your voice to work with. It's a small step, so don't worry about videos you've already posted. Your family code word (step 21) is the real protection.

### 5. Don't send money, gift cards or crypto, or change bank details, because of pressure

**Why this matters:** Money sent by **wire transfer** (sent straight from your bank to another bank), **Zelle** or other instant payment apps, **gift cards**, **cryptocurrency** (digital money, like Bitcoin) or **cash** is very hard to get back. That's why criminals ask for it. At work, the biggest losses come from a simple email that changes where an invoice gets paid.

**What it looks like:**
- Anyone asking you to pay with **gift cards**. They'll ask you to read out the numbers on the back, or send a photo of the card. **No real business or government office takes gift cards to pay a bill.** Ever.
- Anyone asking you to put cash into a **Bitcoin ATM** (a machine, often in a shop or gas station, that turns cash into Bitcoin), or to "move your money to a safe account" to protect it.
- Anyone asking you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to a courier, or leave it for pickup. No real bank, government agency or police officer ever does this.
- "Keep this between us." "Don't tell your bank why you're sending it." Secrecy is a giant warning sign.
- An email from a supplier, client, landlord or title company: "We've changed banks. Please use our new account details for this payment." The email may come from the real person's hacked account, or an address that's one letter different.
- An email "from an employee" asking whoever runs payroll to change their direct-deposit account. Check by phone or in person, the same as a supplier.
- An email from "the boss," often while they're travelling: "I need you to handle a payment quickly and quietly," or "Send me everyone's W-2 forms" (or a client file).
- A new online friend or "investment coach" who shows you amazing profits and encourages you to invest more. You often meet them through a "wrong number" text, a dating app or social media.
- An online job that pays you for simple "tasks" (liking videos, rating products), then asks you to deposit money to unlock your earnings.
- A buyer or new "employer" who "accidentally" pays you too much, often by check, and asks you to send back the difference. The check bounces days or weeks later, and the money you sent is gone.

**Do this instead:**
- **Never pay with gift cards, crypto or a Bitcoin ATM because someone asked you to.**
- **Any change to bank details is checked by phone, on a number you already had.** Call the supplier or client on the number from an old invoice, your contacts or their official website. Never use the phone number in the email that asked for the change.
- At work, follow your office's payment rules, even if "the boss" says to skip them. A real boss will thank you for checking.
- Before you send money because of a call or message, **stop and talk to someone you trust** first (step 20).

**If it already happened:**
- **Wire or bank transfer:** call your bank **right now**. Ask for the fraud team and say, "I've been scammed and I need to recall this payment." Every hour counts. Then report it to the FBI at **IC3.gov** the same day. The best chance of getting money back is in the first two or three days.
- **Zelle, Venmo, Cash App or PayPal:** report it in the app and call your bank or the app's support (found inside the app) right away.
- **Gift cards:** call the company that issued the card on the number on the back of the card, and tell them it was used in a scam. Keep the card and the receipt.
- **Crypto:** contact the company you used to send it, and report it to the FBI at **IC3.gov**.
- **A work payment:** contact IT and your manager straight away.
- Report every case at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov** (see step 26).

**Going further:**
- **Set lower limits in your bank's app.** Many banking apps let you lower the daily limit for Zelle and transfers, and some let you block wires until you call in. Then a criminal who gets into your account, or talks you into a payment, can only move a small amount. The trade-off: for a big payment like a house deposit, you'll need to raise the limit or visit the branch first.
- **Name a trusted contact on investment and retirement accounts.** Brokerages (like Fidelity, Schwab or Vanguard) let you name a **trusted contact person**. If staff think you're being scammed, or they can't reach you, they can call that person. That person can't move your money or make trades. This is especially useful for older parents.
- **If you run your office's payments, write down a two-person rule.** Any payment over an amount you choose, and every change of bank details, needs a second person to check it, plus a call to a number you already had. Paying to new bank details waits one working day. Write it down so nobody has to argue with "the boss" in the moment. The trade-off is a slightly slower process, which is the point. If you don't set the rules, follow your office's (see "Do this instead" above).
- **Stop bank accounts being opened in your name.** Besides the three credit bureaus (step 24), banks often check a separate service called **ChexSystems** when someone opens a checking account. You can place a free security freeze at **chexsystems.com**. The trade-off: you have to lift it before you open a new bank account yourself.

### 6. Don't let anyone remote-control your computer or phone

**Why this matters:** "Remote access" lets someone else see your screen and use your computer from far away. Once a criminal is in, they can open your bank, install harmful software and steal files while you watch.

**What it looks like:**
- A pop-up says **"Your computer is infected! Call Microsoft (or Apple) Support now"**, maybe with a loud alarm. It looks scary, but it's just a web page.
- A call from "Microsoft," "Apple," "Amazon," "your internet company" or even "IT," saying they've found a problem and need to connect to fix it.
- They ask you to install an app like **AnyDesk**, **TeamViewer**, **Quick Assist** or **ScreenConnect**, or to share your screen on a video or FaceTime call.
- After connecting, they "accidentally" refund you too much and ask you to send the difference back.

**Do this instead:**
- Only allow remote access if **you** called IT, on the number you already know (`714-392-3287`), and you're expecting it. **If anyone calls you first and asks for remote access, even "IT," hang up and call IT back on `714-392-3287`.** Apple, Microsoft and your bank won't call you out of the blue to ask for it. At home, only let someone you know and trust help with your computer, in person or on a call you started.
- If a scary pop-up appears, don't call the number. It can't hurt you if you just close it. Close the browser (the app you use for websites, like Safari, Chrome or Edge). **Mac:** press **Command + Q**. If that doesn't work, press **Option + Command + Esc**, choose the browser, and click **Force Quit**. **Windows:** press **Ctrl + Shift + Esc** to open Task Manager, choose the browser, and click **End task**. **iPhone:** swipe up from the very bottom of the screen and pause halfway. You'll see your open apps. Push Safari up and off the top of the screen.
- When you open the browser again, if it asks to "reopen" or "restore" the pages you had open, tap **No** or **Cancel**. On a Mac, hold **Shift** while you open Safari so it doesn't reopen the old windows.

**If it already happened:**
1. Disconnect from the internet. **Mac:** click the Wi-Fi symbol (the fan of curved lines) at the top right of the screen and turn Wi-Fi off. **Windows:** click the Wi-Fi symbol at the bottom right and turn it off. Or unplug the internet cable. This cuts them off.
2. Don't use the computer for banking or email until it's been checked.
3. From a **different** device, change your important passwords (email first, then bank).
4. Call your bank on the number on your card.
5. Contact IT (or your trusted helper). IT will check the computer and remove anything the scammer installed.

**Going further:**
- **Check what remote access your Mac already allows.** The **Mac guide, step 7** walks through **System Settings > General > Sharing**. At home, **Screen Sharing**, **Remote Management** and **Remote Login** should be off unless someone you trust set them up on purpose. Then look in **System Settings > Privacy & Security** under **Accessibility** and **Screen & System Audio Recording**: these list apps allowed to control or see your screen. On a work Mac, don't change anything here: IT may use these to support you. If something looks wrong, take a screenshot and ask IT.
- **Remove remote-control apps you don't use.** On a home computer, look in your apps for **AnyDesk**, **TeamViewer**, **ScreenConnect**, **LogMeIn** or similar. If you didn't knowingly install it, or you no longer use it, remove it. A scammer who got in once may have left one behind to come back later. If you're not sure how to remove it fully, ask your trusted helper. On a work computer, ask IT before removing anything.
- **Agree in advance how your helper connects.** Pick one way, with one person. For example: "My son helps me with FaceTime screen sharing, and only when I call him." Then any other app, person or "technician" asking to connect is wrong by definition. It's easy to remember, and easy to say no.
- **After a scare, check for hidden profiles.** On iPhone, a scammer may have talked you into installing a profile that can change your settings or send your internet traffic through them. See the **iPhone guide, step 13a** to check for anything unknown. On a work iPhone, IT's own profile is supposed to be there, so ask IT before removing anything.

### 7. Don't follow "fix it" instructions or install things from a pop-up

**Why this matters:** A newer trick gets **you** to run the criminal's software for them. A web page gives you a few "easy steps" to prove you're human or fix an error. Those steps actually paste and run a harmful command. Because you ran it yourself, your security software may not stop it.

**What it looks like:**

You don't need to know what these words mean. If a web page asks you to do any of these, it's a scam.

- A fake "I'm not a robot" check or error page that tells you to press keys like **Windows key + R** (or open File Explorer, PowerShell or Terminal), then **Ctrl + V** (or Command + V), then **Enter**.
- A page that tells Mac users to open **Terminal** (a Mac app for typing computer commands) and paste a command to "fix" something or "verify" themselves.
- A pop-up or ad saying you need a **"required update"**, a **video player**, a **codec** (extra software a fake site says you need to watch a video), a **browser extension** or a **"security scan"** to keep going.
- A message saying your browser is "out of date" with a download button.
- A website asking you to **Allow notifications** so you can "continue" or "prove you're not a robot." Tap **Don't Allow**. Allowing it lets the site send fake virus alerts later. If that's already happened, remove the site in your browser's notification settings.

**Do this instead:** **No real website ever asks you to press special keys, open Terminal or the Run box, or paste anything to prove you're human.** If you see this, close the page. Only install apps from the **App Store** or the software maker's own website, and only when you went looking for them. Your Mac or iPhone updates itself through **Software Update**, never through a web page.

**Heads-up:** On a Mac, see the **Mac guide, step 9** (only allow apps from the App Store and known developers) and **step 13** (review browser extensions). A few optional checks in the **Mac guide** (steps 2, 6, 9 and 18) use Terminal. Those are different: you chose to do them, and you type them yourself from that guide. Never paste a command that a web page, pop-up, email or caller gives you.

**If it already happened:** If you pressed the keys or pasted a command, disconnect from the internet, stop using the computer and contact IT (or your trusted helper) straight away. From a different device, change your email and bank passwords.

**Going further:**
- **Use an everyday account that can't install things.** On a Mac, the **Mac guide, step 21** shows how to use a standard (non-admin) account for daily life. Then installers and system changes have to ask for a separate admin password, which gives you a clear moment to stop and think. It doesn't stop everything: some harmful software runs without admin rights, and some shows a fake password box, so the rule above still applies. The trade-off: you type that admin password a little more often.
- **Block the ads that carry these traps.** Many fake "update" and "I'm not a robot" pages arrive through ads on normal websites. In Firefox, **uBlock Origin** is the well-known free blocker. Chrome only allows the lighter **uBlock Origin Lite**. For Safari, look in the App Store for a content blocker such as **AdGuard** or **1Blocker**. Add only one, from the official store, and review it with your other extensions (**Mac guide, step 13**; **iPhone guide, step 23**). Some sites will complain or break, and you can switch the blocker off for those sites. On a work computer, ask IT first: they may not allow extensions.
- **Install software from the maker, on purpose.** When you want an app that isn't in the App Store, type the maker's web address yourself (step 18). Don't follow an ad or a search result. The first time you open it, your Mac should say Apple checked it for harmful software. If your Mac warns it can't check the app, or you're told to change security settings or click **Open Anyway** to open it, stop. On a work computer, ask IT before installing anything.
- **Clean out notification permissions now and then.** Safari on Mac: **Safari > Settings > Websites > Notifications**. Chrome: **Settings > Privacy and security > Site settings > Notifications**. Remove any site you don't recognise. Sites that got permission once can keep sending fake virus alerts for years.

### 8. Don't search for a support number and call the top result

**Why this matters:** Criminals pay for ads and fake listings so their phone number shows up first when you search "Apple support," "bank phone number" or "airline customer service." You call, and the "agent" is a scammer.

**What it looks like:**
- Search results at the very top marked **Sponsored** or **Ad**.
- A phone number in a search summary, an **AI answer** (from Google, a chatbot or a voice assistant), a map listing or a social media comment.
- An "agent" who asks for remote access, gift cards, your password or a verification code.

**Do this instead:** Get support numbers from places you can trust:
- The back of your card, or your printed bill or statement.
- The company's official app (look for **Help**, **Support** or **Contact us**).
- The company's website, reached by typing the address yourself (step 18).
- For Apple, the **Apple Support** app, or **support.apple.com** typed into your browser.

**If it already happened:** If you gave them anything (card numbers, passwords, codes or remote access), follow "If it already happened" in steps 2 and 6, and call your bank.

**Going further:**
- **Save the real numbers now, while you're calm.** Look up support numbers for your bank, cards, phone company, internet provider, airline loyalty program and Apple (from the places listed above) and save them in your contacts. Add a note saying where you got each one, like "from back of Visa." When something goes wrong, you never need to search.
- **Start support from inside the app.** Many banks and companies offer **chat** or a **"call me back"** button inside their own app. Because you're already signed in, they know it's you, and you know it's them. It often skips the hold music too.
- **Know what real support never asks for.** Real agents don't ask for your password, remote access you didn't ask for, or payment by gift card or crypto. If an "agent" asks for any of these, hang up, even if you called them. You may have reached a fake number. Call back on a number from your saved list.
- **Be extra careful with AI answers and voice assistants.** When you ask a chatbot or a smart speaker for "the phone number for X," it may repeat a scam number it found online, and it sounds just as confident either way. Use AI to find the company's **website address** at most, then type it in yourself and find the number on the company's own Contact page.

### 9. Don't reuse passwords or use easy ones

**Why this matters:** Websites get hacked all the time, and the stolen passwords get published. Criminals then try those same email-and-password pairs on banks, email and shopping sites, automatically. If you use one password everywhere, one leak opens everything.

**What it looks like:**
- The same password (or the same password with a different number at the end) on several sites.
- Passwords like **Password1!**, **123456**, a pet's name, a child's name and birth year, or your street name.
- A password written on a sticky note on your screen.

**Do this instead:** Use a **different password for every account**, and let a password manager create and remember them for you (step 15). Where a site offers a **passkey** (a newer way to sign in with your face, fingerprint or device passcode instead of a password, see step 15), use it. If you must make a password up yourself, use four or more random, unrelated words together (not a saying, song lyric or quote), like "lamp otter receipt purple." Longer is stronger. Don't use an example from a guide, including this one.

**If it already happened:** Start with your email account and your bank. Give each one a new, unique password. The Passwords app (step 15) and the breach check (step 25) show you which other passwords to change next.

**Going further:**
- **Make your few memorised passwords with dice.** You only need to remember a handful: your password manager, your phone or computer, and maybe your email. For those, the **EFF dice word list** (search "EFF dice-generated passphrases") lets you roll real dice to pick words, so the result is truly random and not something you'd naturally choose. Six words is a strong goal for your password manager. It takes about 10 minutes, and a few days of typing it before it sticks.
- **Close accounts you no longer use.** Every old forum, shop or app account is one more place your details can leak from. When the Passwords app or a breach check (step 25) shows an account you don't need, sign in and delete it, rather than just changing its password. Some sites hide the delete option, so search the site's help pages for "delete account."
- **Use a different email address for sign-ups.** With **Hide My Email** or **SimpleLogin** (see step 1), each site gets its own email address as well as its own password. A leak from one site then gives criminals neither your real email nor a password that works anywhere else. The trade-off: your mail depends on that service, so pick one you'll keep, and don't use an alias for your bank or your Apple Account.
- **Get told when a new leak happens.** At **haveibeenpwned.com** you can sign up for a free email alert when your address appears in a new breach (step 25). Then change the password for that site. Because no two of your passwords are the same, that leak doesn't open your other accounts.

### 10. Don't scan QR codes without checking where they go

**Why this matters:** A QR code is just a link you can't read. Criminals stick their own QR codes over real ones, or print them in letters and emails, to send you to fake payment pages.

**What it looks like:**
- A sticker on a **parking meter**, an **electric car charger** or a **restaurant table**, sometimes stuck over the real code.
- A flyer or letter you weren't expecting, or an email that says "scan to view your document" or "scan to keep your account active."
- A package you didn't order with a QR code inside "to find out who sent it."

**Do this instead:** Before you open a QR code, read the web address your phone shows. On iPhone, the **Camera** app shows the address in a small yellow label before it opens anything. If the address doesn't match the company you expect (step 19 shows how to read an address), don't open it. For parking and payments, use the official app or the meter itself instead. Be extra careful with QR codes on stickers.

**If it already happened:** If you entered card details, call your bank on the number on your card. If you entered a password, change it on the real site.

**Going further:**
- **At work, never scan a QR code from a work email with your personal phone.** Your office's scam filters check links in emails, but they can't easily read a link hidden in a picture. Your personal phone also has none of the work protections. That's exactly why criminals use QR codes. Report the email to IT instead (step 26).
- **Make scanning a deliberate choice.** On iPhone you can turn off **Settings > Camera > Scan QR Codes**, then use the **Code Scanner** in Control Center only when you mean to scan. Trade-off: one extra step at restaurants and car parks.
- **Check a doubtful link without opening it.** A link-checking site can visit the page for you. Step 19's **Going further** shows how to do it privately. Never check a link that's personal to you, like a password-reset or document-sharing link.
- **Touch the sticker.** On a parking meter or charger, run your finger over the code. If it's a sticker stuck on top of the printed code, or the edges peel, walk away and pay in the official app.

### 11. Don't sign in on a computer that isn't yours

**Why this matters:** A hotel lobby computer, a library PC or a friend's laptop can have hidden software that records everything you type. Even if it's clean, the next person can open your still-signed-in email.

**What it looks like:**
- Checking your email or bank on a shared computer at a hotel, airport, library or print shop.
- Signing in to your Apple Account, Google or Microsoft on a friend's laptop "just for a minute."
- A box asking **Trust this browser?**, **Remember this device?** or **Stay signed in?**

**Do this instead:** Use your own phone for anything important, even if it means using your phone's own internet instead of the shop's Wi-Fi. If you really have to use another computer, open a private window so the computer doesn't keep you signed in. On Windows, click the three dots or three lines at the top right of the browser and choose **New Incognito window**, **New InPrivate window** or **New private window**. On a Mac, it's in the **File** menu. A private window doesn't stop hidden recording software, so still avoid email, banking and work accounts on computers you don't control. Don't tick **Trust**, **Remember me** or **Stay signed in**. Don't let it save your password. Sign out when you finish. For printing, email the file to the business, or use a USB stick, rather than signing in to your email there.

**Heads-up:** This is the same idea as never tapping **Trust This Computer** when you plug your iPhone into someone else's computer. See the **iPhone guide, step 16**.

**If it already happened:** From your own device, change that account's password, and use the account's security settings to sign out of all other devices or sessions.

**Going further:**
- **Use your phone's internet, not the venue's computer.** Turn on **Personal Hotspot** on your iPhone and use your own laptop or tablet instead of a lobby PC. Trade-off: it uses your mobile data and battery.
- **If you must sign in elsewhere, use a passkey from your phone.** On many sites you can choose to sign in with a passkey, then pick the option for a phone or another device. The computer shows a QR code that you scan with your iPhone, and your password is never typed, so recording software can't capture it. If the computer then offers to save a passkey or password, say no. Trade-off: Bluetooth has to be on and the phone near the computer. You're still signed in on that computer until you sign out, so the step's rule about email, banking and work accounts still stands.
- **On a borrowed computer, use Guest mode.** In Chrome, a **Guest** window can't see the owner's saved passwords, bookmarks or history, and throws away your browsing when you close it. Like a private window, it doesn't stop recording software.
- **Do a session sweep afterwards.** Back on your own device, open the account's list of devices or sessions and sign out the computer you used. For Apple, Google and Microsoft accounts this is on their security pages. Do it the same day.
- **Travelling a lot?** Some people carry a separate, cheap, fully updated travel phone or tablet with only the accounts they need for the trip. Trade-off: one more device to keep updated and passcode-locked.

### 12. Don't trust a site just because it has a padlock or a great deal

**Why this matters:** The padlock next to the web address (or **https** at the start of it) only means the connection is private. It doesn't mean the site is honest. Criminals get padlocks for free. Fake shops can look just as polished as real ones.

**What it looks like:**
- A designer bag, a new phone or concert tickets for half price, on a site you've never heard of.
- A shop you found through a social media ad, with a countdown timer: "Only 2 left!"
- A web address that's almost right: **amaz0n-deals.shop**, **apple-support-help.com**, **paypal.secure-login.co**.
- Payment only by bank transfer, Zelle, crypto or gift card.

**Do this instead:** Shop at stores you know, by typing their address or using their app. If a deal is too good to be true, it is. For a store you don't know, search its name plus "scam" or "reviews" before you buy. Pay with a credit card or Apple Pay (step 23). Turn on **Fraudulent Website Warning** in Safari: on iPhone, see the **iPhone guide, step 14**. On Mac, open **Safari > Settings > Security** and tick **Warn when visiting a fraudulent website**.

**If it already happened:** Call your card company on the number on your card and ask to dispute the charge. Report the site at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov**.

**Going further:**
- **Use a different card number for every shop.** A **virtual card number** works like your real card but can be cancelled on its own. **Apple Card** has a virtual number in Wallet that you can replace, some banks (for example Capital One) can make a separate number for each shop, and **Privacy.com** gives free card numbers you can lock to one shop or use once (12 new cards a month on the free plan, US only). Trade-off: Privacy.com takes money straight from your bank account like a debit card, so the protection is weaker than a credit card's (step 23).
- **Check how old the website is.** Type the shop's address into **lookup.icann.org** and look for the date it was created. A "brand" whose website was made a few weeks ago is a big warning sign. The owner's name is usually hidden, which is normal.
- **Block ads, which is where many fake shops find you.** On a personal computer, **uBlock Origin** in Firefox is the strongest free ad blocker. In Chrome and Edge, use **uBlock Origin Lite**. In Safari, use a content blocker from the App Store, such as **AdGuard** or **1Blocker**. Trade-off: some sites break or ask you to switch it off. On a work computer, **ask IT first**.
- **Turn on Chrome's stronger warnings.** In Chrome, **Settings > Privacy and security > Security > Enhanced protection** checks pages against Google's list of dangerous sites as you browse. Trade-off: it sends more of your browsing to Google.

### 13. Don't overshare online, or send private photos

**Why this matters:** Criminals use what you post to guess your passwords and security answers, time a break-in while you're away, or make a scam call sound believable ("Hi, I know you're in Italy right now...").

**What it looks like:**
- Posting "Off to Hawaii for two weeks!" while you're still away.
- Fun quizzes like "Your rock star name is your first pet plus the street you grew up on." Those are common security questions.
- Photos of boarding passes, tickets, driver's licenses, passports, new house keys or checks. Barcodes and numbers in photos can be read.
- Public posts that show your kids' school, your daily routine or your home address.
- Someone you met online, often pretending to be a girl or boy your age, asks for a private photo. Then they threaten to send it to your friends and family unless you pay or send more. This is called **sextortion**, and it targets teenagers most.

**Do this instead:** Post holiday photos **after** you get home. Skip the quizzes. Never post photos of documents or barcodes. Set your social media accounts to private or friends-only. Every app hides this setting in a different place, usually under **Privacy**. This is a good one to do together with a family member. For security questions, you don't have to give the real answer. Make up an answer and save it in your password manager (step 15).

**Heads-up:** If anyone threatens to share private photos: **don't pay**. Paying only brings more demands. Stop replying, but don't delete the messages: they're evidence. Block and report the account in the app. Tell a parent or another adult you trust. You're the victim of a crime, and you're not in trouble. Parents: say this to your teenagers now, before it happens.

**If it already happened:**
- **You overshared:** delete the post. If you shared a photo of an ID or a card, watch your accounts closely and think about a credit freeze (step 24).
- **Someone is threatening to share a private photo:** report it to the FBI at **tips.fbi.gov**. If the person in the photo is under 18, also report it at **report.cybertip.org**, and use **takeitdown.ncmec.org** to help get the images removed from websites. Adults can use **stopncii.org**. None of these services need you to send them the photo.

**Going further:**
- **Give sign-ups a different email address.** Apple's **Hide My Email** (part of a paid iCloud+ plan, and free when you use **Sign in with Apple**) or a free alias service such as **SimpleLogin** or **Firefox Relay** creates a random address that forwards to you. If a shop leaks or sells it, you switch that one address off. Trade-off: one more thing to look after, and the free plans limit how many addresses you get.
- **Remove location from photos before sharing.** On iPhone, in the Share screen, tap **Options** at the top and turn off **Location**. Photos can carry the exact spot they were taken, including your home.
- **Get yourself off people-search sites.** Data brokers sell your address, phone number and relatives' names, which scammers use to sound believable. California residents can send one free request to every registered broker through the state's **DROP** site (privacy.ca.gov). Elsewhere, paid services like **DeleteMe**, **Optery** or **Incogni** do it for you, or you can opt out site by site for free. Google's **Results about you** can alert you when your phone number or home address shows up in search, and lets you ask for removal. Trade-off: outside California, brokers tend to add you back, so it's a yearly job.
- **Clean up the past, not just the future.** Old public posts can still show your kids' school and your routine. Many social apps let you change who can see all your past posts at once (Facebook calls this **Limit past posts**).

### 14. Don't paste private or client information into AI chatbots or free tools

**Why this matters:** Free AI chatbots, PDF converters, file compressors and "free" online tools may keep what you upload, use it to teach their software, or let their staff read it. Some fake "free converter" sites also hand you back a file with harmful software inside.

**What it looks like:**
- Pasting a client email, a contract or a spreadsheet into a free AI chatbot to "make it sound better" or "summarise it."
- Uploading a bank statement, tax return or medical letter to a free website to "convert PDF to Word."
- Installing a "free" browser extension that promises to help with AI, coupons or downloads.

**Do this instead:** For work, only use the AI and file tools your office has approved. If you're not sure, ask IT first. At home, take out names, account numbers, addresses and anything private before you paste. To convert or combine PDFs on a Mac, the built-in **Preview** app can do a lot of this without uploading anything.

**If it already happened:** If it was client or work information, tell IT and your manager. It's much better to say so early. If the site let you download a file, don't open it. Delete it.

**Going further:**
- **Turn off training in the chatbots you use at home.** In ChatGPT, go to **Settings > Data Controls** and turn off **Improve the model for everyone**, or use a **Temporary Chat**. Claude and Gemini have similar settings in their privacy menus. Trade-off: this only affects new chats, the company may still keep your chats for a while, and pressing thumbs up or down can send that chat to the company anyway.
- **Redact properly, don't just cover.** A black box drawn over text in a PDF can often be removed, leaving the text readable. On a Mac, open the file in **Preview**, choose **Tools > Redact**, select the text, and save. That deletes the hidden text for good, so keep a copy of the original first.
- **Watch out for AI browser extensions and AI "note-takers."** Many can read every page you open, or join and record your calls. Treat them like remote access: only install ones you've chosen yourself, and on work computers **ask IT first**.
- **For sensitive personal documents, prefer AI that stays on your device.** **Apple Intelligence** does many tasks on the device itself. Anything it hands to **ChatGPT** goes to OpenAI, so leave the ChatGPT option off or say no when it asks. Confident users can run a local AI app such as **LM Studio** or **Ollama** on a personal computer, so nothing leaves it. Trade-off: local AI needs a fairly powerful computer, is less capable, and is still software you install. On work computers, use only what IT has approved.

## Part 2: Do's

### 15. Use a password manager (and passkeys)

**Why this matters:** Nobody can remember 100 different strong passwords. A password manager remembers them for you, makes new ones with one tap, and only fills them in on the **real** site. That last part quietly protects you from fake login pages.

**Steps**

Apple devices come with a free password manager called **Passwords**. If your office gives you a different password manager, use that one for work accounts instead.

1. **iPhone:** open the **Passwords** app. (It's built in on iOS 18 and later. On older versions, look in **Settings > Passwords**.)
2. **Mac:** open the **Passwords** app from **Applications**, or press Command and the space bar together and type "Passwords". (It's built in on macOS 15 Sequoia and later.)
3. **iPhone:** make sure AutoFill is on. Go to **Settings > General > AutoFill & Passwords**. Turn on **AutoFill Passwords and Passkeys** and make sure **Passwords** is ticked.
4. **Mac:** make sure AutoFill is on. Go to **System Settings > General > AutoFill & Passwords**, and turn on **AutoFill Passwords and Passkeys**.
5. Make sure your passwords are shared between your iPhone, iPad and Mac. **iPhone:** **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**, and turn on **Passwords** (it may be called **Passwords and Keychain**). **Mac:** **System Settings > [your name] > iCloud**, and turn on **Passwords** (it may be called **Passwords and Keychain**).
6. Next time you sign up for something, let Safari or the app **suggest a strong password**, and tap **Use Strong Password**.
7. When you sign in to an old account, change its password and let the Passwords app save the new one. Start with email and banking.
8. When a site offers **"Create a passkey"** or **"Sign in with a passkey,"** say yes. A passkey uses your Face ID, Touch ID or device passcode instead of a password. It can't be stolen by a fake page, because it only works on the real site.

**No Apple devices?** Android phones and the Chrome browser have a free password manager built in, called **Google Password Manager**. It saves and fills in passwords the same way. To see your passwords, type **passwords.google.com** into your browser. Its **Checkup** (sometimes called **Password Checkup**) shows passwords that are reused, weak or leaked. If your household mixes Apple, Android and Windows, see **Going further** below.

**If you don't see this:** open **Settings** (iPhone) or **System Settings** (Mac) and type "AutoFill" or "iCloud" in the search box at the top.

**How to check it worked:** Open the **Passwords** app. Your accounts are listed. Tap **Security** in the Passwords app to see passwords that are reused, weak, or showed up in a leak.

**Heads-up:** The Passwords app is protected by your device passcode and your Apple Account. That's why a strong passcode, two-factor and Stolen Device Protection (**iPhone guide, steps 3, 6 and 8**) and a strong Mac login password (**Mac guide, step 4**) matter so much.

**Going further:**
- **Plan for the day you can't.** Whatever you choose, write the master password (or 1Password's **Emergency Kit**) on paper and keep it somewhere safe that a trusted person knows about. A short list of which accounts you have and where the recovery codes are kept helps too. Keep the passwords themselves in the password manager, not on that list.
- **Know where Apple Passwords stops.** It's end-to-end encrypted and a great choice for a household that only uses Apple devices. But there's no app for Android or Linux. On Windows it works only through the **iCloud for Windows** app and a browser extension. You can only share passwords (**shared groups**) with people who use Apple devices. There's no emergency access built in: Apple's **Legacy Contact** can get your photos and files after you die, but **not** your passwords or passkeys. And the vault opens with your device passcode, so you can't make it need a physical security key.
- **For mixed households or small offices, use a dedicated manager.** **1Password** and **Bitwarden** work on every phone and computer, let you keep separate shared vaults (for example "Family" and "Office"), and let you protect the account with a **security key** (step 16). With **1Password Families**, an organiser can help a member who gets locked out. Bitwarden's paid plans add **emergency access** for a trusted person. Trade-off: 1Password costs money, and Bitwarden's extras need its low-cost Premium or Families plan. Moving across is easy because Passwords can export your list. Delete the export file straight away, because it isn't encrypted.
- **Want a vault that never touches anyone's cloud?** **KeePassXC** (free) keeps your passwords in one encrypted file on your own computer, but syncing, backups and remembering its master password are then entirely your job.
- **Use Obsidian (or any notes app) as a notebook, never as the safe.** An Obsidian vault is just a folder of ordinary text files with no password of its own, so anyone who gets into your computer can read it. It's great for the "where things are" list above: which accounts you have, which email each uses, where the recovery codes are kept. Keep the actual passwords, codes and recovery keys in the password manager.

### 16. Turn on two-factor authentication

**Why this matters:** Two-factor (also called 2FA, two-step verification or multi-factor) means that signing in needs your password **plus** something else, like a code or your phone. A stolen password alone is no longer enough.

**Steps**

Do these first: your **email**, your **bank and credit cards**, your **Apple Account**, your **phone company** account, and **social media** (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, LinkedIn).

1. Sign in to the account, using its official app or by typing its address.
2. Look for **Security**, **Sign-in & security**, **Login settings** or **Privacy & security**.
3. Find **Two-factor authentication**, **2-step verification** or **Multi-factor authentication**, and turn it on.
4. Choose an option. If you're unsure, choose **text message**. It's much better than nothing, and you can change it later. From strongest to weakest:
   - A **passkey** or **security key** (a small physical key you plug in or tap) is best.
   - An **authenticator app** (a free app that shows a new 6-digit code every 30 seconds), for example Google Authenticator or Microsoft Authenticator. Or use the codes feature in Apple's **Passwords** app: open the account, tap **Edit**, then **Set Up Verification Code**, and scan the QR code the website shows you.
   - A **text message code** (better than nothing).
5. Save the **backup codes** or **recovery codes** it gives you (spare codes you can use if you lose your phone) in your password manager, or print them and keep them somewhere safe at home.
6. **Lock your phone number.** Sign in to your phone company's app or website and turn on its free number lock. **T-Mobile** calls it **SIM Protection** and **Port Out Protection**, **Verizon** calls it **Number Lock** and **SIM Protection**, and **AT&T** calls it **Wireless Account Lock**. Also set an account PIN that isn't your birthday or part of your Social Security number. This works for any phone. Before you switch phones or carriers, you'll need to turn the lock off. The **iPhone guide, step 11b** has more detail.

For your Apple Account, see the **iPhone guide, step 6a**.

**If you don't see this:** every website puts it somewhere different. Try searching the site's help pages for "two-factor," or ask IT or a trusted helper to look with you.

**How to check it worked:** In the same security settings, two-factor now says **On**.

**Heads-up:** Text message codes can be stolen if a criminal takes over your phone number. That's why an authenticator app or passkey is better, and why locking your number (item 6 above) is worth doing. And remember step 2: a code is never for sharing.

**Going further:**
- **Get two security keys.** A physical key such as a **YubiKey** (Yubico's basic **Security Key** costs about $25 to $30) is the hardest sign-in method to phish. Add **two** keys to your email, Apple, Google, Microsoft and password-manager accounts, and keep the spare at home. For your Apple Account, read the **iPhone guide, step 31** first, including its downsides. If you use that Apple Account on any device you use for work, ask IT first. Trade-off: if you lose both keys and your other ways in, you can be locked out for good.
- **Close the weak back door.** Once a passkey, security key or authenticator app is set up, see whether the account lets you remove **text message** as a backup option. Then a criminal who steals your number gets nothing. Some accounts, including your Apple Account, always keep a phone number, and that's what the number lock in item 6 above is for. Trade-off: fewer ways back in if you lose your phone, so save your backup codes first.
- **Decide where your codes live, on purpose.** Keeping codes in your password manager is convenient, but if someone breaks into that one app, they get both locks at once. Some confident users keep codes in a separate authenticator app with its own encrypted backup, such as **2FAS** or **Ente Auth** (both free). Trade-off: one more app to back up and move to a new phone.
- **High-risk? Use the strongest settings.** Journalists, executives, people in public roles and anyone being targeted can join Google's free **Advanced Protection Program**, which requires a passkey or security key to sign in. Trade-off: some outside apps lose access to your Google account, and account recovery is deliberately slow.

### 17. Protect your email account most of all

**Why this matters:** Your email is the master key. Almost every other account (bank, Amazon, Apple, social media) lets you reset its password by email. Whoever controls your email can take over everything else.

**Steps**

1. Give your email account a strong, unique password that you use **nowhere else** (step 15).
2. Turn on two-factor, preferably with a passkey or authenticator app (step 16).
3. Check your **recovery phone number** and **recovery email address** in the account's security settings. Make sure they're yours and up to date. Remove any you don't recognise.
4. Check the list of **devices** or **recent activity**. Sign out anything you don't recognise.
5. In your email's settings, look for **Forwarding** and **Filters** (or **Rules**). Delete any you didn't set up. Criminals add hidden rules that quietly forward your mail, or hide replies from you. (Gmail: **Settings > See all settings > Filters** and **Forwarding**. Outlook: **Settings > Mail > Rules** and **Forwarding**. iCloud Mail: **Settings > Mail Forwarding** and **Rules**.)
6. Look for **Third-party apps**, **Connected apps** or **Apps with access to your account**. Remove anything you don't recognise or no longer use. An app you gave access to keeps it even after you change your password.

**If you don't see this:** ask IT or a trusted helper to look with you. It only takes a few minutes.

**How to check it worked:** Your recovery details are yours, the device list only shows your devices, there are no forwarding rules or filters you don't recognise, and only apps you know have access.

**Heads-up:** For a work email account, IT looks after these settings. If you see a forwarding rule, a connected app or a device you don't recognise on your work email, don't change it. Take a screenshot and contact IT.

**Going further:**
- **Use a secret email address for the accounts that matter.** Create a separate address that you use only for your bank, Apple Account, password manager, and as the recovery address for your main email. Never put it on business cards, sign-up forms or social media. Phishing can't reach an address criminals don't know. Trade-off: one more inbox to check now and then, and it needs the same strong password and two-factor as your main email.
- **Run the built-in security checkups twice a year.** **Google Security Checkup** (myaccount.google.com/security-checkup), the **Security** page of your Microsoft account (account.microsoft.com/security), and **Sign-In & Security** for your Apple Account (in **Settings > [your name]**) walk you through devices, recovery details and connected apps in a few minutes.
- **Go passwordless where you can.** Personal Microsoft accounts can have the password removed completely, so you sign in with a passkey or the Authenticator app. Google lets you use a passkey instead of your password. There's nothing to phish and nothing to leak. Trade-off: before you switch, make sure you have a second way in (another passkey, a security key or backup codes).
- **Own your own email domain? Never let it lapse.** Some people use an email address on their own web address (for example through iCloud+ or Fastmail). Turn on auto-renew and the registrar's **transfer lock** for that domain. If it expires, anyone can buy it and receive your password-reset emails.
- **Keep a copy of your email.** Download a backup now and then (for Gmail, use **Google Takeout**). If the account is ever taken over or locked, you still have your records. Store the download somewhere safe, because it holds everything.

### 18. Go to sites yourself: type the address, use a bookmark or the app

**Why this matters:** If you never tap links in messages to sign in, a fake login page can't catch you. This one habit stops most phishing.

**Do this instead:**
- Use the company's **official app** for banking, shopping, deliveries and tolls.
- **Bookmark** the sites you sign in to often (your bank, email, insurance), and always use the bookmark.
- Or **type the address** carefully yourself, like **irs.gov** or the address printed on your bank card or statement.
- In search results, skip anything marked **Sponsored** or **Ad** when you're looking for a login page or phone number (step 8).

**Steps: bookmark a site in Safari**

1. Go to the real site by typing its address.
2. **iPhone:** tap the **Share** button (a square with an arrow pointing up). If you don't see it, tap the three dots (**...**) first. Then tap **Add Bookmark** or **Add to Favorites**.
3. **Mac:** press **Command + D**.
4. Next time, open it from your bookmarks or Favorites.

**If you don't see this:** in another browser, look for a star symbol next to the address, or ask a trusted helper to set up your bookmarks with you.

**How to check it worked:** Your bank, email and other important sites are in your bookmarks or Favorites, and you use them from there.

**Heads-up:** Your bank's app and your password manager both help here. The app always goes to the real bank. The password manager only offers to fill in your password on the real site. If it doesn't offer, stop and look at the address.

**Going further:**
- **Treat your password manager as a tripwire.** If it doesn't offer to fill in your password, don't copy and paste it in. Stop and check the address. This one rule stops even very convincing fake sites, and passkeys do the same automatically. Trade-off: now and then a real company signs you in on a different address. If that happens, check the address against your statement or the official app before you save it.
- **Give money its own browser.** Make a separate browser profile with only your bank and card bookmarks and no extensions. Chrome, Edge and Safari (on Mac and iPhone) all call these **Profiles**. Some people go further and keep one fully updated iPad only for banking. Trade-off: a little more switching around.
- **Filter dangerous sites for the whole house.** A filtering DNS service quietly blocks known scam and malware sites for every device on your home Wi-Fi. **Quad9** (9.9.9.9) is free and simple. **NextDNS** gives you more control (free up to 300,000 lookups a month, then it stops filtering until the next month unless you pay about $2 a month). You can set it once in your home Wi-Fi router. Trade-off: now and then it blocks a real site, and it doesn't replace your own checks. On work computers and phones, don't change these settings. If IT has given you a safe-browsing profile (**iPhone guide, step 13b**), leave it in place.
- **Skip search for the sites you use.** In the address bar, type the first few letters of the bookmark's name and pick the bookmark from the list. The fewer times you search for "my bank login," the fewer chances a paid ad has to catch you.

### 19. Check before you tap: preview links and sender addresses

**Why this matters:** The words in a message can say anything, like "Apple Support" or "Your Bank." The real web address and the real sender address are much harder to fake.

**Steps: check where a link goes**

1. **iPhone (Mail, Messages, Safari):** press your finger on the link and keep it there for a second or two. Don't tap. A preview appears with the web address at the top. Read it, then tap outside the preview to close it.
2. If the preview shows the page itself, tap **Hide Preview** at the top. Your iPhone remembers this, so from then on it shows just the address. If you don't see **Hide Preview**, only read the address, and don't tap or type anything inside the preview.
3. **Mac (Mail):** move the pointer over the link, **without clicking**, and wait a moment. The real address appears in a small pop-up.
4. **Mac (Safari):** click **View** in the menu at the top of the screen, then **Show Status Bar**. You only do this once. After that, when you rest the pointer on a link, its real address appears at the bottom of the window.

**How to read the address:** Find the first single slash (**/**) after the name. Just before that slash is the real website. Read it from right to left. The name right before **.com**, **.org**, **.gov** (or a country ending like **.co.uk**) is the real site. It must match the company's name **exactly**. **apple.com** is Apple. **apple-support-help.com** and **app1e.com** are not. **apple.com/support** goes to Apple. **apple.com.account-verify.io/support** goes to **account-verify.io**, so it's not Apple, even though "apple" appears at the start. A familiar name anywhere else in the address means nothing. If you're not sure, don't tap. Go to the site yourself (step 18).

**Steps: check who an email is really from**

1. **iPhone (Mail):** tap the sender's name at the top of the email. The full email address appears.
2. **Mac (Mail):** move the pointer over the sender's name, or click the small arrow next to it, to see the full address.
3. Look for small changes: **paypa1.com**, **micros0ft-support.com**, a free Gmail address for a "bank," or a client's name with a slightly different company address.

**How to check it worked:** Press and hold (iPhone) or rest the pointer on (Mac) a link in an email from a company you trust. You can see the real web address before you open it, and you can see the sender's full email address.

**Heads-up:**
- A correct-looking address isn't proof. A real person's email account can be hacked. If a message asks for money, a code, a password or a change of bank details, check by phone anyway (step 5).
- If a text from an unknown sender says "reply Y to activate the link" or "reply STOP," don't reply. Replying turns the link on and tells them your number is real.

**Going further:**
- **Read the email's hidden "envelope."** Every email carries technical notes, called headers, that show whether it passed the sending company's own checks. In Gmail on a computer, open the message, click the three dots and choose **Show original**. Near the top you'll see **SPF**, **DKIM** and **DMARC**, each marked **PASS** or **FAIL**. In Mail on a Mac, choose **View > Message > All Headers** and find the line that starts **Authentication-Results**. A **FAIL** on a message claiming to be your bank is a strong warning. A **PASS** only means the email really came from the domain after the @, and criminals own lookalike domains too.
- **Check a link without opening it.** Press and hold the link, choose **Copy Link**, and paste it into **urlscan.io** or **virustotal.com**. These free sites visit the page on their own computers and show you a screenshot, where the link really ends up, and whether security companies flag it. Trade-off: what you submit can be seen by others. On urlscan, choose **Private** before you scan, and never submit a link that contains your name, an account number or a personal sign-in link.
- **Watch for letters that only look the same.** Some fake addresses swap in a letter from another alphabet, such as a Cyrillic "а" for a normal "a", so the address looks exactly like **apple.com**. You often can't spot this by eye. That's why going to the site yourself (step 18) beats checking the address.
- **Short links and QR codes hide the address.** Links like **bit.ly/...** don't show where they go, so treat them as unknown. On iPhone, the Camera app shows a QR code's web address in a small yellow label before you tap it. Read it the same way as any other address (step 10).

### 20. Slow down when something feels urgent

**Why this matters:** Almost every scam uses the same pressure: **hurry**, **fear**, **secrecy** or **excitement**. Strong feelings make smart people skip their normal checks. That's the whole trick.

**What it looks like:**
- "Act now," "within 24 hours," "or your account will be closed."
- "Don't tell anyone," "don't hang up," "don't talk to your bank."
- "You've won," "you're owed a refund," "this investment can't lose."
- A threat: arrest, a lawsuit, losing your account, a loved one in trouble.

**Do this instead:** Give yourself a **10-minute rule**. Before you pay, click, install, or share anything because of a message or call, stop. Hang up or close the message. Then check it in a way **you** choose (your own number, your own app, your own bookmark), and talk it over with someone you trust: a family member, a colleague, or IT. A real problem will still be there in 10 minutes. A scam usually falls apart.

**Heads-up:** If someone says "Don't hang up" or "Stay on the line while you go to the bank," that alone tells you it's a scam.

**Going further:**
- **Make your rules before the pressure starts.** Write down a few rules while you're calm, and keep the list by your computer. For example: "I never pay anyone by gift card, wire or crypto because of a call," or "Any payment over $1,000 waits a day and gets checked by a second person." Under pressure, following a rule you already made is easier than thinking clearly.
- **Add slowdowns at your bank.** Ask your bank whether you can lower your daily **Zelle** and transfer limits, whether wires can require a call-back or a branch visit, and whether you can set a spoken password for phone requests. What's offered varies by bank. Trade-off: a real large payment takes a little longer.
- **Name a trusted contact on your investment accounts.** US brokerages ask for a **trusted contact person** they can call if they see signs of fraud or confusion. This person can't make trades or move your money. Add one in your account settings, or call the brokerage.
- **At work: two people for money and bank changes.** Every new payee, change of bank details, or urgent payment gets approved by a second person, who checks by calling a number already on file. The rule also protects whoever gets the call, because "I'm not allowed to do that alone" is an easy thing to say.

### 21. Set up a family (and office) code word

**Why this matters:** AI can copy a voice from a short video clip, and caller ID can be faked. A secret word that only your family knows is a simple way to check that a desperate call is real.

**Steps**

1. Pick a word or short phrase that's easy to remember but impossible to guess. Not a pet's name, street, birthday or anything you've posted online. For example, "blue pancake" or "Uncle Walter's canoe." Don't use these examples. Make up your own.
2. Tell your family **in person** (or on a call you started). Don't post it, text it in a group chat, or write it on the fridge.
3. Agree on the rule: **anyone who calls or messages asking for money, help or secrets has to say the code word.** No word, no money, no matter how real the voice sounds.
4. Teach it to older parents and teenagers too. They're common targets.
5. **At work:** agree with your manager and colleagues that any unusual or urgent request for payment, gift cards, bank detail changes or passwords gets checked by calling back on a known number, or with a code word your office chooses.

**How to check it worked:** Ask a family member, "What's our code word?" They know it, and they know the rule.

**Heads-up:**
- If a caller can't give the word, hang up and call your family member on the number you already have. If the code word ever leaks, pick a new one.
- Talk to teenagers about the private-photo threat scam in step 13 at the same time. If it happens, they should stop replying and tell you right away. They won't be in trouble.

**Going further:**
- **Use a question and an answer, not just one word.** One person asks a question ("How's the garden?") and the other gives an answer that makes no sense to anyone else ("The goats ate it"). Even someone who overheard the question doesn't know the answer. Make up your own pair.
- **Change it on a schedule.** Pick a new word or pair once a year, and any time someone who knew it leaves the family circle or the office. Agree on the new one in person.
- **Write down your office's call-back numbers.** Keep a short printed list of known numbers for your manager, finance, IT and your main suppliers, and a copy on your phone. When an "urgent" request comes in, call back on the list's number, never a number in the message or email signature.

### 22. Keep your devices, browser and apps updated

**Why this matters:** Updates fix the security holes criminals use. An out-of-date phone, computer or browser can be attacked just by visiting the wrong web page.

**Steps**

1. **iPhone:** follow the **iPhone guide**, "Before you start" and steps 1 and 15 (automatic updates for iOS and apps).
2. **Mac:** follow the **Mac guide**, steps 0 and 1 (automatic updates for macOS and App Store apps).
3. **Browser:** Safari updates with your iPhone or Mac. If you use **Chrome**, **Edge** or **Firefox**, they update themselves when you close and reopen them. If they show an **Update** or **Relaunch** button, click it.
4. **Windows PC, Android phone, tablet, smart TV, home Wi-Fi router:** turn on automatic updates in their settings if you can.
5. When a device no longer gets updates, plan to replace it. You'll know because it's many years old and stays on an old version, even though it says it's up to date. Ask IT or a trusted helper if you're not sure.

**How to check it worked:** Every device says it's up to date. On iPhone: **Settings > General > Software Update**. On Mac: **System Settings > General > Software Update**.

**Heads-up:**
- Real updates come from your device's own **Software Update** screen or the **App Store**, never from a pop-up on a website (step 7).
- Public Wi-Fi at a café or airport is fine for everyday browsing now, because almost all sites encrypt the connection. Be careful with Wi-Fi sign-in pages that ask for your email password or card details, and use your phone's own internet (mobile data) for banking if you're unsure.

**Going further:**
- **Install urgent security fixes the same day.** Apple lists every security update at **support.apple.com/100100**. When the notes say a problem "may have been exploited," criminals are already using it. Don't wait for automatic updates. Go to **Software Update** and install it now. On a work device, follow IT's update schedule if they have one.
- **Know when a device stops getting fixes.** Phones, computers, routers and smart TVs all reach an end date. The website **endoflife.date** lists support end dates for many products. A device that's no longer supported still works, but new holes in it are never fixed. Keep it away from your banking and email, or replace it.
- **Check your home Wi-Fi router.** Routers often go years without updates. Sign in to its app or settings page, turn on automatic updates (often called **firmware** updates), and change the admin password if it's still the one on the sticker. If your internet company gave you the router, ask them whether it still gets updates. Trade-off: a router that updates itself sometimes restarts overnight.
- **Fewer extras means fewer holes.** Remove browser add-ons (extensions) and apps you don't use. Extensions can be sold to new owners who quietly add harmful code in an update. Keep only one or two you really trust. On work devices, ask IT first before adding or removing anything.
- **Give smart gadgets their own network.** Cameras, smart plugs and TVs often stop getting updates early. If your router has a **Guest** network, put them on it, so a hacked gadget can't easily reach your computer. Trade-off: your phone may not be able to control or cast to them while it's on your main network.

### 23. Pay with a credit card, and turn on purchase alerts

**Why this matters:** In the US, credit cards generally give you stronger protection against fraud than debit cards. With a credit card, a criminal is spending the bank's money while you dispute it. With a debit card, it's your money that's missing. **Apple Pay** is even safer, because the shop never sees your real card number.

**Steps: pay safely**

1. Online, pay with a **credit card**, **Apple Pay** or **Google Pay**. Avoid debit cards for online shopping.
2. **Never** pay a stranger, a new online shop or a "seller" on social media by **wire transfer**, **Zelle**, **Venmo**, **Cash App**, **PayPal Friends and Family**, **gift card** or **crypto**. These apps are for people you know and trust, like paying your sister back for dinner. If a buyer or seller says you must "upgrade to a business account" to receive money, it's a scam.

**Steps: turn on alerts**

1. Open your **bank's app** and each **credit card's app**.
2. Look for **Alerts**, **Notifications** or **Security** in the settings or profile.
3. Turn on alerts for **every purchase**, or for purchases over a small amount like $1. Also turn on alerts for **online purchases**, **international purchases**, **new people or companies added to your account to send money to**, **password changes** and **new devices**.
4. Choose app notifications or texts, whichever you'll actually see.

**If you don't see this:** every bank's app is different. Look for a gear symbol or your name, or call the number on your card and ask them to turn on purchase alerts for you.

**How to check it worked:** Buy something small, like a coffee. You get an alert within a few minutes.

**Heads-up:** An alert you didn't expect means: open your bank app, or call the number on your card. **Don't** reply to a text "from your bank" with a link or phone number in it. Criminals send fake alerts too.

**If it already happened:** If you see a charge you don't recognise, lock or freeze the card in your bank's app if it offers that, and call the number on the back of the card.

**Going further:**
- **Use virtual card numbers for online shops and subscriptions.** A virtual card number is a stand-in number that you can lock to one shop, limit, or delete. If that shop is hacked, you delete the number and your real card is untouched. **Capital One** offers virtual numbers for its credit cards, and **Apple Card** lets you request a new card number in the **Wallet** app at any time. Ask your own card company what it offers. **Privacy.com** links to a US bank account, and its free plan includes up to 12 new cards a month. Trade-off: Privacy.com takes money straight from your bank account, so you don't get the credit-card protection described above.
- **Keep one card just for online.** Use one credit card with a low limit for all online shopping and subscriptions, and keep your main card for in-person spending. If the online card is stolen, the damage is capped and you only have to update one set of accounts.
- **Lock cards you rarely use.** Most card apps have a **Lock** or **Freeze** switch. Keep backup cards locked and unlock them only when you need them.
- **Use a separate browser profile for money.** In Chrome, Edge or Firefox you can create a second **profile** just for banking: no extensions, no other sites, just a bookmark to your bank. In Safari on a Mac, choose **Safari > Settings > Profiles**. Trade-off: you have to remember to switch profiles. On a work computer, check with IT first.

### 24. Freeze your credit with all three bureaus

**Why this matters:** The three credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) are companies that keep a record of your loans and credit cards. A credit freeze stops anyone (including you, until you lift it) from opening a new credit card, loan or account in your name. It's **free**, it doesn't hurt your credit score, and it's one of the best protections against identity theft.

**Steps**

You need to do this with **each** of the three bureaus. Go to their real websites by typing the address yourself:

1. **Equifax:** go to **equifax.com** and look for **Credit Freeze** or **Security Freeze**.
2. **Experian:** go to **experian.com** and look for **Freeze**.
3. **TransUnion:** go to **transunion.com** and look for **Credit Freeze**.
4. At each one, create a free account and place the freeze. You'll need your name, address, date of birth and Social Security number. That's normal **on these sites, which you went to yourself**.
5. Save each login, and any PIN they give you, in your password manager.
6. Help your partner and adult family members do their own (each adult needs their own account at each bureau). You can also freeze credit for children under 16, which protects them from identity theft before they ever use credit. Each bureau's site explains how. It usually means sending proof of identity by mail.

**How to check it worked:** Each bureau's account shows your credit as **frozen**.

**Heads-up:**
- You can still use your existing cards and accounts normally.
- Before you apply for a loan, credit card, mortgage or car loan, lift the freeze for a short time. This is called a "thaw." Some phone companies and landlords check your credit too. Sign in, choose **temporarily lift**, and pick the dates. You can do this at the bureau they use, or at all three.
- By law, a freeze you ask for online or by phone must be in place within one business day. Lifting it (a thaw) online or by phone must take effect within one hour.
- The bureaus may offer paid "credit lock" or monitoring products. The free **freeze** is all you need.
- These sites aren't the government. If a page asks for payment just to place a freeze, you're on the wrong page.
- For extra protection, get a free **IRS Identity Protection PIN** at **irs.gov/ippin** (it stops someone filing taxes in your name), and create your own **my Social Security** account at **ssa.gov** so nobody else can create one as you.

**Going further:**
- **Freeze the fourth bureau, Innovis.** Some lenders use **Innovis**, a smaller credit bureau. A freeze there is free too. Type **innovis.com** yourself and look for **Security Freeze**. Save the PIN or login in your password manager.
- **Freeze your banking and phone reports.** **ChexSystems** keeps reports that banks check before opening accounts, and **NCTUE** keeps reports that phone, cable and utility companies check. Both offer free freezes on their own sites (**chexsystems.com** and **nctue.com**). Trade-off: lift them before you open a new bank account or set up new phone or utility service.
- **Lock your Social Security number in E-Verify.** Many US employers use **E-Verify** to check that new hires can work. Create a free **myE-Verify** account at **e-verify.gov** and turn on **Self Lock**, so your number gets flagged if someone else uses it there. It only covers employers that use E-Verify. It lasts a year and you can extend it. Unlock it before you start a new job.
- **If your Social Security number was stolen, block online access.** Call Social Security at **1-800-772-1213** and ask them to **block electronic access** to your record. Then nobody, including you, can see or change your details online or through their automated phone line. Trade-off: to undo it, you have to prove who you are, by phone or in person. Only do this if you know your number was stolen.
- **Stop pre-approved credit offers.** Type **optoutprescreen.com** to stop the "you're pre-approved" credit card letters that criminals can steal from your mailbox and use. It's the official site run by the credit bureaus. You can opt out for five years online, or permanently by mailing a signed form.

### 25. Check if your email was in a data breach

**Why this matters:** If a site you use was hacked, your email and password may be in a list that criminals share. Changing those passwords makes the stolen list useless.

**Steps**

1. Type **haveibeenpwned.com** into your browser. (The odd spelling, "pwned," is on purpose. It's a free, well-known service run by a security expert. It only needs your email address, never your password.)
2. Type your email address and click the button next to it (it may say **Check**).
3. It lists the hacked sites where your email appeared, and what was taken.
4. For each one you still use, change the password to a new, unique one (step 15) and turn on two-factor (step 16).
5. If the same password was used anywhere else, change it there too.
6. Also open the **Passwords** app and tap **Security**. It lists your passwords that are reused, weak, or known to be leaked. Work through that list, starting with email and money accounts. (On Android or in Chrome: type **passwords.google.com** and open **Checkup**.)

**How to check it worked:** The Passwords app's **Security** list is empty, or only has accounts you've decided don't matter.

**Heads-up:**
- Being on the list is very common, and it doesn't mean you did anything wrong. It just means it's time to change those passwords. Be careful of emails claiming "your data was found in a breach, click here." Go to the site yourself instead.
- You may get an email that shows one of your old passwords and claims they hacked your camera and will share videos unless you pay in crypto. It's a bluff built from a data breach. Don't reply or pay. Change that password if you still use it, and delete the email.

**Going further:**
- **Get told about the next breach automatically.** On **haveibeenpwned.com**, click **Notify me** and enter each email address you use. It's free, and it emails you if that address shows up in a future breach. You don't have to remember to check.
- **Give each site its own email address.** **Hide My Email** (part of iCloud+, in **Settings > [your name] > iCloud**) makes a random address for each sign-up that forwards to your real inbox. **SimpleLogin** and **Firefox Relay** do the same on any device, with a few addresses free. When a site is hacked or starts spamming, you turn off that one address. A fake "your account" email also stands out, because it arrives at the wrong address. Trade-off: you need to save which address you used (your password manager can do that), and if you stop paying for the service, check that your addresses will still forward. Keep your real address for banks, Apple and your email's recovery settings.
- **Switch your most important accounts to passkeys.** A passkey can't be stolen from a website's database and reused, so a breach at that site doesn't hand criminals a password for you. Start with email, Apple, Google, Microsoft and your bank if they offer it (step 15).
- **Get your details off people-search sites.** Data brokers sell your address, phone number and relatives' names, which scammers use to sound believable. California residents can ask every registered data broker to delete their data in one free request through the state's **DROP** site, found at **privacy.ca.gov**. Elsewhere, services like **DeleteMe** or **Optery** send removal requests for you, mostly for a yearly fee. Trade-off: removals are never complete, and some details come back and need removing again.

### 26. Report scams

**Why this matters:** Reports help stop the scam for the next person, help police track the criminals, and are often needed if you want your bank to refund you.

**Steps**

1. **Scam email at work:** don't click anything, and don't forward it to colleagues. Forward it only to IT (`support@twentynineeleven.net`), or use the **Report phishing** button if your email program has one. Then delete it.
2. **Scam text:** forward it to **7726** (the numbers spell "SPAM"). It helps your phone company find and block scam senders. On iPhone, you can also tap **Report Junk** under the message. Then delete it.
3. **Scam email at home:** use **Report spam** or **Report phishing** in your email app, or move it to **Junk**.
4. **Any scam or fraud, even if you didn't lose money:** report it to the FTC at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov**.
5. **Internet crime where you lost money** (wire, crypto, online shopping, investment scams): also report it to the FBI at **IC3.gov**.
6. **Someone used your identity** (opened accounts, filed taxes in your name): go to **IdentityTheft.gov** for a step-by-step recovery plan.
7. **Older adults (60+) or their family:** the free **National Elder Fraud Hotline** at **1-833-372-8311** helps with reporting and next steps. The **AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline** at **877-908-3360** is free for anyone.

**How to check it worked:** You get a confirmation or reference number. Save it with your notes about what happened.

**Heads-up:** These official sites never call you first or ask for payment. Be careful of "recovery" companies that contact you promising to get your lost money back for a fee. That's a second scam aimed at people who've already been scammed.

**Going further:**
- **Keep the evidence before you delete it.** Screenshots lose the hidden details. For an email, save a full copy first: in Mail on a Mac, choose **File > Save As** and pick **Raw Message Source**. In Gmail on a computer, use **Show original**, then **Download original**. Write down the scammer's phone numbers, web addresses and payment details. Banks, police and IT can do much more with these than with a description. At work, do what IT asks instead.
- **Report the fake website so browsers block it.** Report a phishing page to Google at **safebrowsing.google.com/safebrowsing/report_phish/**. Chrome, Firefox and Safari all use Google's list of dangerous sites, so one report can protect many people. You can also forward phishing emails to **reportphishing@apwg.org**, a group that shares them with security companies.
- **Tell the company being faked.** Most big companies have a report address or page, such as **reportphishing@apple.com** for fake Apple messages. Find it on the company's own website. They can get fake sites taken down faster than you can.
- **Get a police report number for big losses.** If you lost a lot of money or your identity was used, also file a report with your local police. Banks and insurers often ask for one, along with your **IdentityTheft.gov** report.
- **Keep a simple incident log.** At home or at work, keep one note for each incident: the date, what happened, who you told, and the reference numbers. It saves time when the bank calls back weeks later, and it helps your family or office spot the same scam next time.

### If you think you've been scammed

First: **don't be embarrassed.** These scams are run by professionals, and they catch smart, careful people every day: doctors, lawyers, accountants and security experts. The faster you act, the more you can save. Here's what to do in the first hour.

1. **Stop.** Hang up the call, stop replying, and don't send anything else, even if they threaten you or promise to "fix" it.
2. **Disconnect.** If they had remote access or you installed something, turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the internet cable on that device (step 6 shows how).
3. **Call your bank** and card companies, on the number **on the back of your card** or in their official app. Tell them what happened. Ask them to stop or reverse any payments, and to lock or replace your cards.
4. **Change your passwords from a different device**, one the scammer never touched. Start with your email, then your bank, then your Apple Account. Turn on two-factor if it isn't on.
5. **Contact IT** on `714-392-3287` or `support@twentynineeleven.net`. Do this for anything that touched a work account, work device or client information, and for any personal device you also use for work. IT would much rather hear from you in the first five minutes than find out a week later. No IT department? Call your trusted helper and go through these steps together.
6. **Write down what happened** while you remember it: times, phone numbers, web addresses, names they used, amounts and how you paid. Take screenshots.
7. **Report it** (step 26): ReportFraud.ftc.gov, IC3.gov if you lost money, IdentityTheft.gov if your identity was used. If you're 60 or older, the National Elder Fraud Hotline (1-833-372-8311) can help.
8. **Think about a credit freeze** (step 24) if you gave out your Social Security number, date of birth or a photo of your ID.

**Heads-up:** Tell your family. Not because you did something wrong, but because the same criminals often try the same trick on the people around you.

### You're done

Great work. You now know the tricks criminals use most, and you've set up the protections that stop them.

**The three rules to remember:**
- **Never share a code.** Not with anyone.
- **Hang up and call back** on a number you look up yourself.
- **Slow down.** Urgency and secrecy are the signs of a scam.

**Every few months**, take 10 minutes to:
- Check the **Security** list in the Passwords app.
- Look at your bank and card statements for anything odd.
- Remind your family about the code word.
- Check that your phone and computer are backed up (iCloud Backup, Time Machine, or what IT provides), so a scam or broken device can't take your photos and files. See the **iPhone guide, step 2** and the **Mac guide, step 19**.

If anything in this guide didn't match what you see on your device, or you're not sure whether something is a scam, contact IT (or your trusted helper). Tell them which step number. A screenshot helps a lot. There are no silly questions.
